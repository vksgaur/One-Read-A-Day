The Clause He Argued Away: B. N. Rau, Felix Frankfurter and the Phrase India Did Not Borrow

On 19 November 1947, a civil servant from Mangalore called on a Justice of the United States Supreme Court in Washington. The meeting was courteous, and by most accounts it left a mark on one of the most consequential sentences in the Indian Constitution.

The visitor was Sir Benegal Narsing Rau, Constitutional Adviser to India's Constituent Assembly. The Justice was Felix Frankfurter. The sentence is Article 21: "No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law." The words it does not contain, "due process of law," were in Rau's own first draft. Their removal shaped how India's courts would treat liberty for nearly three decades.

The adviser nobody remembers

Benegal Narsing Rau was born in Mangalore on 26 February 1887 and educated at Cambridge before entering the Indian Civil Service. He served as a district judge and later as a judge of the Calcutta High Court, helped with the drafting work behind the Government of India Act, 1935, and was Prime Minister of Jammu and Kashmir from 1944 to 1945. In July 1946 he became Constitutional Adviser to the Constituent Assembly, where, according to one tribute, he worked honorarily.

He was not a member of the Assembly and he was not its public face. Jawaharlal Nehru's description of him survives: "I doubt if anyone saw him ruffled at all." Rajendra Prasad said Rau was the person who "visualized the plan and laid the foundation." On 25 November 1949, in his closing speech, B. R. Ambedkar said that the credit given to him did not really belong to him, and that Rau had "prepared a rough draft of the Constitution for the consideration of Drafting Committee." Ambedkar gave a still larger share to the Chief Draftsman, S. N. Mukherjee.

Rau finished that draft in late October 1947. It is worth noticing what he did next. Instead of waiting for the Drafting Committee, he left for the United States, Canada, Ireland and the United Kingdom, to talk with the people who had lived with such texts. His list included Justice Frankfurter, Judge Learned Hand and the Irish Foreign Minister. From Ireland India took its Directive Principles of State Policy. From Washington it took a warning.

The phrase that came with a warning

"Due process of law" had been part of the Indian argument long before Rau. In December 1946, during the debate on Nehru's Objectives Resolution, Ambedkar complained that the usual formula, that no man's life, liberty or property shall be taken without due process, had been left out. In March 1947 he put such a clause into his memorandum, States and Minorities, and K. M. Munshi supported him. In April 1947 the Assembly approved a compromise from its Advisory Committee: due process would protect life and liberty, but not property, after Govind Ballabh Pant resisted the broader guarantee.

Rau's draft accordingly carried it. Clause 16 read, in part: "No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty without due process of law." Then came Washington. According to an account in The India Forum, Rau met President Truman on 19 November 1947 and then Frankfurter, who objected to the phrase. Frankfurter's concern, as reported, was that in the American experience it let judges strike down economic and welfare legislation. Another account says he told Rau it "imposes undue burden on the judiciary."

Rau's own objection, as one account describes it, was that due process would give unelected judges a veto over elected legislatures, so Frankfurter's warning fitted a view he already held. The accounts agree that he afterwards urged the drafting bodies to drop the phrase, and he suggested an Irish-style approach in which some rights would be shaped by principles of social justice. Frankfurter wrote to him afterwards that his talks with Rau "were among the pleasantest and most satisfying experiences in Washington."

The vote nobody recorded

What happened next is a small mystery. In January 1948 the Drafting Committee voted to keep the due process language in Rau's clause. Shortly afterwards it reversed itself, and the surviving record shows no discussion and no vote. When the Draft Constitution appeared in February 1948, draft article 15 said "procedure established by law," and the American phrase was gone.

Who changed minds? Granville Austin credited Rau with persuading Sir Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, a leading member of the committee, to support the deletion. Alva, the author of The India Forum essay I am following here, calls Austin's version "largely speculative" in places but still the most plausible. A July 1949 letter from the Home Secretary, H. V. R. Iengar, also traced the change to Frankfurter's opinion reaching the committee through Rau. Ayyar himself argued in the Assembly that it was better to trust the legislature than to let judges second-guess elected representatives. Draft article 15 passed by voice vote.

Ambedkar's position is the most interesting, because he had argued for the clause a year earlier. He accepted that without due process legislatures could trample liberties, but he was unwilling to let unelected judges decide whether a law was good or bad. As a counterweight he introduced draft article 15A, which became Article 22, with its safeguards on arrest and detention. Those safeguards also contained provisions on preventive detention that drew criticism then and have since.

Eleven weeks of the new Constitution

The Constitution came into force on 26 January 1950. A. K. Gopalan, a Communist leader who had been in custody since December 1947, was served on 1 March 1950 with a detention order under the new Preventive Detention Act. He petitioned the Supreme Court. On 19 May 1950 a bench of six, with Chief Justice Harilal Kania writing the main opinion, held that Article 21 did not require Indian courts to apply a due process standard. The Court upheld the Act except for Section 14, which had barred courts from learning the grounds of detention.

Justice Fazl Ali dissented. In his view, "procedure established by law" could not mean any procedure a legislature happened to write; it had to include basic fairness. His dissent was a minority position for twenty-eight years.

The reasoning in the majority was not perverse. The text said "procedure established by law," and the Assembly had chosen those words after a debate in which due process was explicitly rejected. A court that read due process back into them would have been doing what the framers declined to authorise. This is exactly why the case is hard, and why the argument is not simply "Rau was wrong."

The doctrine that came back

In July 1977 the government impounded the passport of Maneka Gandhi, saying only that this was in the public interest, and later gave no reasons. The case reached a seven-judge bench. In 1978, with Justice P. N. Bhagwati writing for a plurality, the Court held that any procedure affecting life or liberty must be "just, fair and reasonable," and that Articles 14, 19 and 21 must be read together as what it called a golden triangle. In doing so it departed from Gopalan, which it overruled. The Court did not formally set aside the passport order, because the Attorney-General said the government would consider a representation from her.

The effect was to restore through interpretation much of what the framers had left out of the text. A phrase deleted in 1948 returned through the courts in 1978, under another name. The irony is easy to state: a clause removed to keep judges in check came back by judicial decision.

What the clause teaches

It would be easy to turn Rau into either hero or villain. He is neither. His fear, that judges armed with open-ended phrases could obstruct a young republic's economic and social laws, had real American history behind it, though one reviewer of a recent book on the framers argues that the doctrine Rau worried about had already been discredited in the United States by the late 1930s. His critics have the stronger practical point: between 1950 and 1978, a person detained under a valid-looking statute had few constitutional arguments about the fairness of the procedure itself.

Three things seem worth carrying away. First, the framers were not copying; they travelled, listened and then chose what not to copy, which is a more serious kind of borrowing than the textbook word suggests. Second, a constitutional text is only the first draft of its meaning, because courts, over decades, write the rest. Third, a man who shaped the document so deeply and who was, in Nehru's phrase, hardly ever ruffled, is now barely remembered. Rau became a judge of the International Court of Justice in 1952, died in Zurich on 30 November 1953, and was honoured by the Lok Sabha with a minute of silence.

His sentence in Article 21 is still the one lawyers argue over. The argument is really about trust: how far to trust legislatures, how far to trust judges, and who is left standing when neither is looking.
