Three Points at Every Raise: How a Pay-Rise Trick Quadrupled Savings

A five-point increase nobody could afford

Sometime in 1998, in a midsize manufacturing company, a financial consultant sat across a desk from an employee and said a number the employee did not want to hear. The company had no traditional pension, so its workers were expected to build their own retirement through a tax-deferred savings plan. The consultant fed each person's details into commercial planning software, and the software's verdict was almost always the same: save much more.

In Richard Thaler's later telling, the advisor was Brian Tarbox, and he began by recommending a rise of five percentage points in the savings rate. Most of the lower-paid workers refused. Five points of pay vanishing from every paycheque felt unaffordable. The advice was sound and the arithmetic was right, and almost nobody followed it.

Then Tarbox tried a different offer. Instead of asking for five points now, he asked for three points at each future pay raise. Of the 207 employees who had turned the first proposal down, 162 said yes. After four raises their average savings rate had gone from 3.5 percent to 13.6 percent.

The programme became known as Save More Tomorrow, and it is one of the cleaner demonstrations that how a request is framed can matter as much as what is requested. This piece looks at why it worked, what happened when it was tried at three companies, how it slipped into American law and practice, what a sceptical economist found when the dust settled, and what the Indian version of the idea looks like on a mutual fund platform today.

Borrowing from a future self

The idea had a longer prelude. In 1994 Thaler had already suggested automatic enrolment in savings plans. Later, a mutual fund company asked him to speak to its clients about raising savings rates, and after talking it over with Shlomo Benartzi, his former student, he settled on a design. In his 2017 Nobel lecture he said it rested on three observations: people show more self-control about plans for the future than about behaviour today, they are loss averse in nominal dollars, and they are strongly inert.

Each observation became a feature. Employees would agree now to a rise that started later, in the first company roughly three months after the advice, because a sacrifice scheduled for later is easier to accept than one that starts tomorrow. The increases would arrive with pay raises, so that no one watched a paycheque shrink. The paper reports that raises at the first company were about 3.25 percent for hourly staff and 3.5 percent for salaried staff, and Thaler told the US Congress that workers under the plan "never have to experience a cut in their take home pay." Finally, the plan ran by itself until the worker reached a ceiling or chose to leave. Doing nothing meant saving more.

It is worth noticing what the design does not do. It does not forbid anything, and it does not penalise anyone for leaving. Employees could opt out at any time, and those who did kept the higher rate they had already reached. Thaler and Benartzi were candid that their aim was modest: to help the employees who already wished they saved more.

Anyone who has watched taxpayers file on the last permitted day will recognise the psychology. Most people do not lack the information about what is good for them. They lack a mechanism that makes the good choice the one that happens when nothing else does.

What the three companies showed

The numbers from the first company come from Thaler and Benartzi's 2004 paper in the Journal of Political Economy. Of 315 eligible employees, all but 29 agreed to meet the consultant. Of the 286 who did, 79 (28 percent) accepted the consultant's own recommended rate. The 207 who would not were offered the new plan, and 162 (78 percent) joined.

Their savings rate climbed with each raise: 3.5 percent before the advice, then 6.5, 9.4, 11.6 and finally 13.6 percent after the fourth raise. By then 80 percent of those who joined were still in. Those who had taken the consultant's advice instead moved from 4.4 to 8.8 percent and stopped. The group that declined every offer started at 6.1 percent and finished at 5.9. Across all 315 employees the average rose from 4.4 to 10.6 percent.

One small honesty about the record. The paper describes the time span of these four raises as 40 months in its abstract, 28 months in one section and 16 months in its conclusions, and a later Chicago Booth article quotes the 11.6 percent figure, which is the third-raise reading, as the result over 28 months. The pattern is unaffected, but anyone quoting the headline should pick the figure and the span from the same place in the paper.

The second trial, in May 2001, was at Ispat Inland, a midwestern steel company with about 5,000 unionised employees. There was no consultant and no seminar, only one letter signed jointly by management and the union, with increments of 2 points per raise and a cap of 18 percent. Among workers already saving but below the cap, 18.1 percent joined. Among those saving nothing, 8.2 percent joined, and after their first raise those joiners were saving 2.28 percent of pay, against 0.26 percent for non-saving workers who stayed out.

The third trial, at Philips Electronics in 2002, tested the pitch itself. Employees could choose increments of 1, 2 or 3 points a year, with 2 as the default; 54 percent picked 1, 35 percent picked 2 and 11 percent picked 3. In the division where seminars were described as required (with no penalty for skipping) and one-to-one meetings with a financial planner were offered, 38.3 percent joined. In the division with optional seminars only, 16.9 percent did. In the first division, 81 percent of those who met the planner signed up. The lesson was uncomfortable for anyone who believes good policy sells itself: the same offer, delivered differently, produced more than double the take-up.

From a factory floor to federal law

Save More Tomorrow did not arrive in an empty field. Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea had studied a large American company that switched new hires from opt-in to automatic enrolment, and found that participation more than doubled among them. But the default was cautious: 76 percent of the automatically enrolled contributed at the 3 percent default, and fifteen months later about three-quarters of them still held their entire balance in the default money market fund. The average contribution of automatically enrolled hires was 4.4 percent of pay, against 6.4 percent across all participants. Madrian drew the obvious conclusion, which was to choose a higher default, or one that rises over time. That is Save More Tomorrow in one sentence.

The Pension Protection Act, signed on 17 August 2006, gave the approach legal cover. It told employers that automatic enrolment was a legitimate plan feature and created safe harbours from nondiscrimination testing and for investing the default contributions. The numbers that followed, from the large recordkeeper Vanguard, are striking. Among new participants at its plans, the share hired under automatic enrolment went from 12 percent in 2006 to 63 percent in 2015. The share of its plans with automatic enrolment was 10 percent about a decade before 2015, 41 percent at the end of that year and 61 percent at the end of 2024. Of the plans that enrol automatically, nearly seven in ten now escalate contributions every year.

The 2024 behaviour is the clearest sign of the idea's reach. Vanguard found that 45 percent of participants raised their deferral rate that year, the highest share it has tracked: 29 percent through automatic annual escalation, 16 percent by their own choice, with 8 percent cutting their rate. Earlier Vanguard research found that people in plans with both automatic enrolment and automatic annual increases saved 20 to 30 percent more after three years. Thaler received the 2017 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, announced on 9 October of that year, "for his contributions to behavioural economics", and the Academy singled out his work on self-control and on nudges that help people save for a pension.

The fair counterweight comes from Taha Choukhmane, whose 2025 paper in the American Economic Review studies the long run. He finds that employees offset much of the short-run gain from automatic enrolment by saving less later, so that its long-run effect on wealth is small for most workers and meaningful mainly for those at the bottom of the earnings distribution. He estimates the cost of opting out at about 250 dollars, far below the thousands of dollars earlier work implied, because people who skip the default can catch up later. That paper concerns automatic enrolment, not escalation, so it does not settle the verdict on Save More Tomorrow itself. It does carry a useful warning for anyone designing a nudge: a default can move the balance in one account on day one by much more than it moves a household's total saving.

The rupee version, and who it is for

India has built a very large saving habit around the systematic investment plan. According to figures reported for August 2026, monthly SIP contributions reached ₹32,297 crore, with about 10.02 crore contributing accounts out of 10.75 crore active SIP accounts. The habit of a fixed sum leaving the account every month is itself a commitment device, a cousin of the idea above.

The closer relative is the step-up SIP, also called SIP top-up. A mutual fund house raises the instalment automatically at fixed intervals, usually yearly, by a percentage or a fixed amount. In an example from Tata Mutual Fund, a ₹5,000 SIP stepped up by 10 percent a year becomes ₹5,500 in the second year and ₹6,050 in the third. AMFI describes a top-up as an option chosen when registering or renewing a SIP, subject to the scheme's facility, and some fund houses require an existing SIP to be cancelled and re-registered to add it.

The differences from Save More Tomorrow are instructive. The Indian feature is a choice made once at the start, with a flat SIP as the ordinary path, whereas Thaler and Benartzi made rising contributions the path of least resistance and tied each step to a pay raise. I did not find a published count of how many Indian SIPs carry a step-up, so I cannot say how often people take it. Treat that as an open and interesting question rather than a finding.

For a salaried household, the transferable idea is small and practical. Decide the increment before the appraisal letter arrives, schedule it to begin with the first higher salary, and let the raise pay for it. This is a description of a behavioural design, not investment advice, and a rising instalment is only as good as the product it flows into.

When the idea was new, Thaler said "We're giving this idea away for free. All we ask for is the data." Two decades of data later, the picture is mixed in the way good evidence usually is. Defaults and escalators reliably get people to act, and the harder question is whether the total saved ends up higher. The 1998 episode is the first half in miniature: a worker who could not say yes to five points today said yes, in large numbers, to three points on a day that had not arrived yet.
