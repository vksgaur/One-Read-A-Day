Bottle 53N: Waldemar Haffkine, the Plague Vaccine and the Trial of a Scientist

On 10 January 1897, in Bombay, a bacteriologist named Waldemar Haffkine took the first dose of his plague vaccine himself. He had made it over roughly three months in a makeshift laboratory in a corridor of Grant Medical College, while the city around him emptied and buried its dead. Nobody yet knew whether it would protect anyone. The only person he could be certain had consented to the risk was himself.

His story is usually told as a triumph, and the first half is one. But the second half is the part worth your time, because it is about what happens to a scientist when a single bottle goes wrong in a village he never visited, and about how long it takes the truth to catch up with an official verdict.

The outsider who kept being the only one willing

Haffkine was born in Odessa. Sources disagree on the year, giving 1856 in one place and 1860 in another, so the birth year is best left open. What is clear is the pattern of his career. In Odessa he studied under Ilya Mechnikov and worked at the zoological museum on single-celled organisms. In 1879, according to Wikipedia's account, he was hurt defending a Jewish home during a pogrom, was arrested, and was freed after Mechnikov intervened. Barred, as a Jew, from a professorship in Russia, he left in 1888 for a post in physiology at the University of Geneva.

In 1889 he joined Mechnikov at the newly founded Pasteur Institute in Paris. The only job on offer was librarian, and he took it. That detail appears in the National Library of Israel's short biography as "the only available opening at the Institute", and it tells you something about both the man and the institution. Within a few years he had moved from protozoa to practical bacteriology, and he did the thing that would define him. On 18 July 1892 he injected himself with a cholera vaccine made from weakened bacteria, and on 30 July he reported the result to the Biological Society in Paris.

France, Germany and Russia were unimpressed. Senior colleagues, Mechnikov and Pasteur among them, did not rush to endorse it. So Haffkine went where the disease was. With the backing of the Marquis of Dufferin, the British ambassador in Paris, he travelled to India in 1893, and began trials in Calcutta during a cholera epidemic. Local medical bodies criticised him at first. The vaccine was later widely accepted. He fell ill with malaria in the autumn of 1895, recovered in England, and returned to India in 1896, just in time for the next catastrophe.

Three months in a corridor

Bubonic plague reached Bombay in October 1896, and the government asked Haffkine for help. He set up in a corridor of Grant Medical College. After about three months he had a vaccine ready for human trials, made by killing a virulent culture at 60 degrees Celsius. It was, by the account on Wikipedia, the first vaccine of its kind for human prevention. On 10 January 1897 he tested it on himself.

Then came the part that mattered scientifically, because a vaccine that has only been tried on its maker proves little. At the Byculla House of Correction, volunteers among the prisoners were inoculated while others were left alone. In a careful later summary published by the James Lind Library, 154 inoculated men had three deaths on the day of inoculation and none in the following six days. Among 191 uninoculated men, three died on the same day and six more over the next six days. Wikipedia's article reports the control-group deaths slightly differently, as seven. Either way the direction was the same, and the numbers were small enough that nobody should call them proof.

The stronger evidence came from communities. Over the next three months more than 11,000 people in infected areas were inoculated. In a village called Undhera in Baroda, households were split, with some members inoculated and others not. Among 71 inoculated people there were eight attacks and three deaths. Among 64 who were not, there were 27 attacks and 26 deaths. That is a striking gap, and it is also a reminder that these were not randomised trials. The author of the Lind Library piece concludes that the vaccine's efficacy "was probably less than the figures from India suggested." Wikipedia adds that protection was incomplete, that the vaccine caused side effects, and that it did not protect against pulmonary plague.

Imperfect or not, it spread. By the turn of the century roughly four million people in India had received it. Haffkine was made a Companion of the Order of the Indian Empire in the 1897 Diamond Jubilee Honours and was given the directorship of what became a permanent laboratory. The Plague Research Laboratory was established on 10 August 1899 at Parel, in the former governor's residence known as Sans Pareil, and opened by Lord Sandhurst, then governor of Bombay. In 1900 he received the University of Edinburgh's Cameron Prize for Therapeutics. A man who had been a librarian in Paris was now running a laboratory in a governor's mansion.

It is worth pausing on how unusual this was. In the 1890s the idea that you could prevent an epidemic disease by injecting dead bacteria was young and contested. A foreigner, trained in a French laboratory, was persuading an imperial administration to inoculate thousands of people on the strength of a handful of trials. The vaccine worked well enough, and the administration believed him. Both of those facts would later become important.

A village in Punjab, a bottle from Bombay

On 30 October 1902, in the village of Mulkowal in Punjab, 107 people were inoculated against plague. Six days later, tetanus appeared among the first 19 who had been injected, and all 19 died. According to the James Lind Library summary, every one of them had received vaccine from a single bottle, numbered 53N, which had been sent from Bombay 26 days earlier. The other 88 people, who received vaccine from different bottles, were unaffected. (Wikipedia dates the incident to March 1902. I have followed the Lind Library, which gives exact dates and cites the official papers.)

Tetanus bacteria live in soil and produce spores that survive heat and chemicals. If they got into a bottle, they would do exactly this. The question was where. Either the vaccine was contaminated in Haffkine's laboratory in Bombay, or something went wrong in Punjab when the bottle was opened and used.

The Government of India appointed a commission of three officers, Jenkins, Bomford and Semple. On 16 April 1903 it reported its opinion that "the specific contamination was introduced before the bottle was opened at Mulkowal." It blamed carelessness in the Bombay laboratory and criticised Haffkine for leaving out carbolic acid, an antiseptic, from the preparation, which departed from the approved method. Haffkine was sent on a year's leave, and in practice his career in Bombay was over. The Wikipedia account says the sterilisation method had been changed from carbolic acid to heating, a method that was new in the British Empire.

This is the point where the story could have ended, and in many histories of colonial medicine it nearly does. A foreign scientist, a regrettable accident, a commission, a departure. What kept it alive was a scientist who would not accept it.

The long argument for the defence

The Lister Institute in London, led in this matter by Charles Martin, was asked to review the case. Its report of 24 November 1904 was ambiguous. Its wording left room to be read as agreeing with the commission. On 5 May 1906 the Institute's Governing Body stated that it regretted having "inadvertently referred to their conclusion as the same as that of the Commission."

Then the full documents were published in the Gazette of India on 1 December 1906, and they changed the picture. They showed that the officer who gave the injections had smelled no odour from the bottle when it was opened. They also showed that the assistant had rinsed the forceps in a carbolic lotion rather than flaming them, as the Punjab Plague Manual instructed. Professor William Simpson wrote to the British Medical Journal making the same point, that the contamination could have happened in Punjab. The difference between a rinse and a flame was, in effect, the difference between two explanations of the same nineteen deaths.

Ronald Ross, who had won the Nobel Prize for his work on malaria, took up the campaign. In a letter to The Times on 15 March 1907 he wrote: "The case as it stands does not convict him of any fault whatever." On 29 July 1907 a second letter, signed by ten bacteriologists, called for public exoneration or a new inquiry. On 29 November 1907 The Times reported a letter from the India Office stating that "an important body of scientific opinion is favourable to him," and the Government offered him employment on honourable terms. He accepted.

Notice the language the India Office chose. It spoke of scientific opinion being favourable and of offering employment on honourable terms, which is the vocabulary of a settlement rather than of a verdict reversed. In 1908 Haffkine became Director-in-Chief of the Calcutta Biological Laboratory, but his work was restricted to research. He wrote that "the whole of the unjust punishment for Mulkowal" still rested on him. Historians have since called it the "Little Dreyfus Affair", and Wikipedia notes that Simon Schama made the comparison. Some accounts link the harshness of the verdict to anti-Semitism. That is an inference about motive that the official papers do not state, and I would treat it as a possibility rather than a finding.

What the bottle teaches

Haffkine retired in 1914 and returned to France, then settled in Lausanne, where he died on 26 October 1930. In 1925 the Bombay laboratory was renamed the Haffkine Institute, and he wrote of it that "the work at Bombay absorbed the best years of my life." The institute still stands at Parel.

The lesson is not that vaccines are risky. It is that systems decide quickly and correct slowly. A commission had a bottle, a number and a deadline, and it found a cause. The evidence that complicated that cause sat in the Punjab records for years until the full papers were published. What reopened the case was a handful of colleagues who read the documents and wrote to a newspaper. For anyone who works inside an administrative system, there is a quiet warning here about how much a first finding becomes a fact simply by being official.

There is a kinder reading as well. A man who had once injected himself to prove a point spent his last decades knowing that the proof of his innocence had been argued by others.
