Nameless on the Roll: Sukumar Sen and the 2.8 Million Women Struck Off

A Question Asked Door to Door

In the months before India's first general election, enumerators across the country carried a simple form and asked every household a simple question: who lives here, and what are their names? For millions of women the honest answer, as custom had taught them, was "wife of" someone, "mother of" someone, or "daughter of" someone. Their own names were something to be kept inside the family.

The man who had to decide what to do with those answers was Sukumar Sen. Born in 1899, he had studied at Presidency College and at London University, where he won a gold medal in mathematics. He joined the Indian Civil Service in 1921, served as a judge, and became chief secretary of Bengal. In March 1950 he was appointed India's first Chief Election Commissioner. Ramachandra Guha, who calls him an unsung hero of Indian democracy, has noted that no officer of the state, and certainly no Indian official, had ever had such a stupendous task placed in front of him.

The scale explains the phrase. By the Election Commission's reckoning the electorate ran to roughly 173 to 176 million adults over twenty-one, depending on which account you read, and about 85 percent of them could not read or write. The rolls had to be compiled from scratch, in a country that had been partitioned only three years earlier and had never held a vote on universal adult franchise.

This article is about one decision inside that vast exercise. Sen's office instructed that the name of an elector, being an essential part of identity, must be included. Women who would not give their own names were not to be registered, and entries that listed only a relationship were to be deleted. Accounts differ on the exact number affected, but the most commonly cited figure is about 2.8 million women. It is a story that does not fit comfortably into either a triumphant or a cynical telling of Indian democracy, which is what makes it worth examining.

The Machine Built Around the Illiterate Voter

To see why Sen was so insistent about names, it helps to see how much of the election was engineered around the idea that every voter had to be a verifiable individual. Guha's account in the Caravan lists the numbers: some 224,000 polling booths, 56,000 presiding officers, 280,000 polling helpers, 16,500 clerks employed just to type and collate the electoral rolls, and about 380,000 reams of paper. Roughly 2 million steel ballot boxes were made, consuming some 8,200 tons of steel. Other accounts give somewhat different counts for booths and boxes, a reminder that even the arithmetic of this election was assembled afterwards from many files. The safest reading is the order of magnitude: it was an operation larger than anything attempted before in any democracy.

The design choices were just as inventive. Because most voters could not read, parties and independents were given pictorial symbols such as bullocks, a hut, an elephant and a lamp. Each candidate had a separate ballot box with the symbol pasted on it, so that a voter simply dropped a blank paper into the box of their choice. The Godrej company made the boxes, and one account credits a shop-floor worker, Nathalal Panchal, with the design. Indelible ink was used to prevent repeat voting, and Guha records that 389,816 phials of it were used. A documentary about the election was shown in more than 3,000 cinemas, and All India Radio ran programmes through 1951.

The first vote was cast on 25 October 1951 in Chini, now in Kinnaur district of Himachal Pradesh. The rest of the country voted in stages, and polling was completed in the early months of 1952. The final turnout was reported as 45.7 percent. The Indian National Congress won 364 of the 489 Lok Sabha seats, with around 45 percent of the votes.

None of this would work if the register could not tell one voter from another. A name is the primary key of any register. A list containing thousands of entries that read only "wife of Ramlal" would invite duplicates, impersonation and disputes on polling day, with no practical way to settle them. That is the administrative logic behind Sen's instruction, and it was not a trivial concern. It was also an uncomfortable fact that the instruction collided with a custom that had real force in homes.

Two Defensible Positions

The decision is easy to condemn in hindsight and just as easy to defend. A careful reader should hold both views at once.

The case against begins with the plain fact that women were kept off the roll. The Constitution had promised universal adult franchise, and the Election Commission, by its own rules, was now refusing some adults the vote because of a social habit they had not chosen. The most affected areas, according to the accounts, were Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Bharat, Rajasthan and Vindhya Pradesh. Women who had been taught that a respectable woman does not say her name to strangers were being asked to break that rule in front of a male enumerator, and were then punished when they declined. Critics could fairly say that a bureaucratic principle was being applied at the expense of the very citizens the franchise was meant to include.

The case for the decision is that Sen saw the roll as a public instrument rather than a favour. He noted that women were "averse to disclosing their proper names to strangers", and he treated that aversion as a relic. Guha's account quotes him calling the practice a "curious senseless relic of the past". The Commission's position was that the name of an elector is an essential part of his or her identity. If the state accepted "wife of" in 1951, the argument went, it would be building a permanent two-tier register in which half the women's identities were derivative. Holding the line was a way of insisting that a vote belongs to a person, not to a household.

It also matters that Sen did not simply close the door. Where the problem was worst, extensions were granted so that women could come forward again under their own names. Bihar received an additional month for applications, which is said to have considerably improved the rolls. Rajasthan was also given an extension, but the response was poor, and many entries still had to be deleted. A former Chief Electoral Officer, Chandra Bhushan Kumar, later described the episode as a very remarkable stand and a tough decision in those days.

The disagreement among sources about the numbers is itself instructive. Most accounts say about 2.8 million women; one newspaper retelling says at least 20 lakh, or 2 million. All agree that the women had been entered by relationship rather than name, and all agree that the Commission refused to count them as registered until that changed. What is contested is not the principle but the scale, and a good reader of any history of this period should check which count is quoted, and from what original document.

What Happened Next

The sequel is the most interesting part. By the second general election in 1957, according to a retelling of Sen's own observations, about 92.1 million women had registered, roughly 94 percent of adult women. Sen reported that the women who had been left out in 1951 were "in fact very disappointed when they saw their sisters exercising their franchise." The custom that had seemed so fixed gave way within a single electoral cycle, once the vote was visibly worth something.

That should make us cautious about both easy readings. It does not prove that the exclusion was harmless, because two million or more women still missed the country's founding vote. But it does suggest that the Commission's firmness and its flexibility worked together. A register that had accepted "wife of" in 1951 might have locked the practice in. A register that insisted on names, while allowing more time and more outreach, gave women a reason and a chance to change what they said at the door.

Sen's career after 1952 shows how far his reputation travelled. Sudan sought his services to conduct its general elections on a universal adult franchise when it went to the polls in 1953. He stayed on as Chief Election Commissioner until December 1958, which meant he oversaw both the first and the second general elections. Guha has noted that he left few papers and no memoirs, so what we know of his reasoning comes mostly from the Commission's own reports and from the administrators who worked under him.

For a student of institutions, the episode offers three lessons that are worth carrying beyond 1951. The first is that a public register is not a neutral list. It encodes a view of who counts as an individual, and every choice about a form or a field is a choice about citizenship. The second is that inclusion and verification pull against each other, and an honest administrator has to say which one is winning on a given day. The third is that a rule enforced with a visible reason, and with room for people to comply later, can change behaviour in ways a rule enforced purely by exclusion cannot.

There is also a question that the record does not fully answer, and it is worth leaving open. Were the 2.8 million women the cost of building a clean register, or were they the price of an administrator's confidence that he knew better than the households he was counting? The evidence supports a measured answer: both motives were present, the Commission tried to soften the blow, and the next election suggests the long-run effect was to widen the franchise rather than narrow it. A reader who prefers a harsher verdict, or a gentler one, will find material for either in the sources below.
