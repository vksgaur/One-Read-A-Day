The Box That Ate the Docks: How a Trucker's Hunch Rewired World Trade

On 26 April 1956 a converted oil tanker called the Ideal-X left Port Newark in New Jersey for Houston, Texas. Besides its usual load of liquid cargo it carried 58 trailer bodies, each 35 feet long, 8 feet wide and 8 feet high. It reached Houston five days later. Nobody on the quay that day was watching the future of world trade, but the future was on that deck.

The man behind it was Malcolm McLean, born on 14 November 1913 in Maxton, North Carolina. In 1935 he, his brother Jim and his sister Clara had started a small trucking firm in Red Springs, hauling empty tobacco barrels. The idea that made him rich, according to the account on Jean-Paul Rodrigue's transport geography site, goes back to 1937, when McLean sat waiting for several days while longshoremen loaded cotton bales onto a ship. A trucker's instinct told him that the slow part of the journey was not the road or the ocean. It was the dock.

That instinct came with a number attached. McLean calculated that loading a medium-sized ship the old way cost about $5.83 a ton in 1956 (Wikipedia gives $5.86), against less than 16 cents a ton for loading containers. That is a saving of roughly 97 per cent. I would treat the exact figure with care, since it is McLean's own estimate and the source page cites nothing for it. But the direction is clear: the cost of moving a ton across a quay fell more than thirtyfold.

This article is about what happened when a cost that large collapsed, and about what it did to ports, workers, economists' models and, eventually, India's customs houses.

The box was the easy part

A steel box is not an invention, and McLean did not pretend otherwise. What he built was a system, and every piece of it had to be fought for.

The engineering came first. According to Wikipedia's account of the intermodal container, Keith Tantlinger, whom McLean hired, had already added steel castings to the top corners of containers in 1949, and for Sea-Land he designed a frame with eight corner castings that could take stacking loads, plus automatic spreaders and the twistlock that grips those castings. The first purpose-built vessel, the converted Gateway City, sailed from Port Newark on 4 October 1957 and, with two onboard cranes, could be unloaded and reloaded in about eight hours.

Then came the ports. Marc Levinson, whose book The Box (2006) remains the standard history, shows that the Port Authority of New York committed to container terminals at Newark and Elizabeth in New Jersey. The old piers in Brooklyn and Manhattan lacked rail links, depended on lighters crossing the harbour and had congested truck access. According to a summary of the book, they declined and much local manufacturing left with them. A technology that rewards flat land, rail and road access does not care about a city's history.

The politics were as hard as the engineering. Marc Levinson's own article on dock labour describes two answers. The International Longshoremen's Association, covering the docks from Maine to Texas, resisted automation and won a guaranteed annual income for members whose hours fell. On the West Coast the ILWU signed its 1960 Mechanization and Modernization Agreement, which allowed unlimited new machinery even where jobs vanished, in return for generous retirement benefits and better pay for those who stayed. Levinson credits that deal with helping Los Angeles and Long Beach become the largest container ports in the United States.

Standards were the slowest battle. Sea-Land's boxes were 35 feet, the length that suited American roads. The wider world settled on 10, 20 and 40 feet, and a review of Levinson's book notes that Sea-Land refused to adopt them at first. The international ISO standards were published between 1968 and 1970 (Wikipedia's dating), and without them a box packed in one country could not have moved by ship, rail and truck across several.

Finally, war paid for the scale. In 1967 the American government invited Sea-Land to open a container service to South Vietnam, and by Wikipedia's account that business produced about 40 per cent of the company's revenue in 1968 and 1969. Levinson adds that filling containers for the return voyage encouraged Japanese containerisation. In 1969 R.J. Reynolds agreed to buy Sea-Land for $530 million in cash and stock; McLean personally made $160 million. He died on 25 May 2001, aged 87.

What the economists found

Trade textbooks carry a quiet assumption. A review of Levinson's book points out that Ricardo's model of comparative advantage assumed transport costs of zero, and that Levinson shows how much they matter. If the cost of crossing a quay falls by a factor of thirty, a great deal of trade that was once pointless becomes profitable.

For decades this was a plausible story told by historians rather than a measured result. In 2015 three economists, Daniel Bernhofen, Zouheir El-Sahli and Richard Kneller, published in the Journal of International Economics what they describe as the first identification strategy for measuring the container revolution's effect on world trade. Their method used the timing of each country's first container facilities, combined with how containerisable each product was. The data ran from 1962 to 1990 and covered 157 countries, with a core sample of 22 industrialised economies.

The headline result is startling. For trade among those 22 countries, adopting containers is associated with a rise of about 320 per cent at once and 790 per cent cumulatively over twenty years. In the same table a free trade agreement scores 26 per cent and 45 per cent, and GATT membership 92 per cent and 285 per cent. The authors say the container effect is "much larger than the effects of free trade agreements or the GATT." One wrinkle is worth recording: the paper's abstract and introduction speak of "about 700%" while its table gives 790%. Across the full sample of 157 countries the container effect is roughly halved, at 357 per cent.

Two cautions apply, and they are mine. These are statistical estimates of how much extra trade followed adoption, not a ledger of what the box alone delivered, and they describe goods that could actually go in a box. And the effect was not instant at the scale of the economy. According to a summary of Levinson's book, the full impact on global manufacturing was not felt until the mid-1980s, about thirty years after the Ideal-X, and by 1986 ports, carriers and shippers worldwide had already invested $76 billion in container systems, with another $130 billion forecast for bigger ships and faster cranes. Levinson calls the savings to shippers and consumers extremely large but impossible to calculate precisely.

The interpretation I would add, as my reading rather than the papers' claim, is about geography. When moving a part across an ocean costs little and happens on a predictable schedule, a factory no longer needs to sit near its customers or its suppliers. The ports that won were not necessarily the grand old ones. According to the same summary, Oakland and Felixstowe rose largely by luck, Singapore and Hong Kong prospered, and Shanghai and Shenzhen have since overtaken them as the busiest.

India arrives late, and then keeps waiting

India's relationship with the box is a story of arriving, building and then discovering that the box moves faster than the paperwork around it.

According to a 2013 paper by G. Raghuram for the International Transport Forum, the Major Port Trust Act of 1963 was followed by containerisation at Calcutta, Madras and Bombay. The big step came with Jawaharlal Nehru Port at Nhava Sheva. Wikipedia dates the port's opening to 26 May 1989, while Raghuram dates the container terminal there to 1988, so the sources differ by a year in a way I cannot settle. What is clear is the scale it reached: Wikipedia reports 5,835,650 TEUs handled in 2023-24, though it also notes a 2021 report that Mundra had overtaken it as India's largest container port. A TEU, the standard unit of the trade, is one 20-foot container.

Still, Raghuram's numbers show how thin the margin for pride was. In 2011-12 the average ship turnaround at India's major ports was 4.47 days, against an international benchmark he calls good of 1.0 day. In 2012 Shanghai handled 32.6 million TEUs and the whole of India about 10 million.

For a customs officer the more interesting figures are about the cargo rather than the ship. The National Time Release Study 2025, the fifth edition, was released by the Finance Ministry on 20 June 2025. Per a TaxGuru summary, average release time for imports at seaports fell by about 6 hours between 2023 and 2025. For exports, regulatory clearance from arrival to the Let Export Order averaged 29 hours 36 minutes, but the logistics stage after that order averaged 157 hours 50 minutes. I am not drawing a policy conclusion from a single summary page, but the pattern is the McLean lesson again. The slow part of the journey is usually not where the regulator is looking. In 1937 it was the longshoremen. Today, at least for exports in this study, it is the time after clearance.

The lesson of a boring standard

There is something almost comic about the way a revolution in global trade arrived. It had no manifesto and no treaty. It was a rectangle, a set of corner castings, a labour contract on each coast and a standards committee that met for years. The Bernhofen study says that this did more for trade among rich countries than the GATT did. That should humble anyone, myself included, who believes trade grows mainly through negotiated tariff cuts.

It should also be kept in proportion. Containers helped some places and hurt others, and Levinson's own account of Brooklyn and Manhattan is about a port, and the work that surrounded it, that simply stopped. The same box that shortened the distance between a factory in one country and a shop in another removed a way of life from a waterfront.

What I find most useful, as someone who spends days among invoices and shipping bills, is the reminder that economic change often hides in the dull parts of the system: a unit of measure, a crane, a form. The 58 trailers on the Ideal-X did not look like history. They looked like a cheaper way for a trucking man to get cargo to Texas.
