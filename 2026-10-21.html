Counting Identical Things: S. N. Bose, Einstein and the Particles That Cannot Be Told Apart

A letter from a stranger

On 4 June 1924, a physicist in Dacca wrote to the most famous scientist alive. He described himself as "a complete stranger". His covering note began: "Respected Sir, I have ventured to send you the accompanying article for your perusal and opinion." A few lines later came the sentence that gives away his nerves: "I am anxious to know what you think of it."

The writer was Satyendra Nath Bose, born in Calcutta on 1 January 1894 and, since 1921, a Reader in physics at the University of Dacca. The recipient was Albert Einstein in Berlin. The enclosure was a five-page paper titled "Planck's Law and the Hypothesis of Light Quanta", which Bose had written in the autumn of 1923. He had first sent it to the Philosophical Magazine in Britain, where it did not get published, so he went to the top and asked Einstein to have it translated into German and placed in a German journal.

Einstein did more than that. On 2 July 1924 he sent back a postcard saying the paper "signifies an important step forward and pleases me very much." He translated it himself and passed it to the Zeitschrift für Physik, where it appeared in August 1924 (volume 26, page 178). He added a footnote of his own: Bose's derivation "appears to me an important step forward. The method used here also yields the quantum theory of an ideal gas, as I shall show elsewhere."

It is worth pausing on how unlikely this chain of events was. Bose taught in Dacca, far from the European laboratories and seminar rooms where the new quantum theory was being argued out. What he had was a single stubborn observation about a piece of physics that everyone else had learned to live with.

The flaw nobody wanted to fix

In 1900, Max Planck had written down a formula for the spectrum of black-body radiation, the glow of a hot object, that matched experiment. The formula worked, but the derivations offered for it were uncomfortable. Planck's law was supposed to signal that classical physics breaks down, yet the derivations in circulation still leaned on classical electrodynamics at some point. Bose noticed this while teaching quantum theory in Dacca. According to a profile in Inference, the problem sharpened when his colleague Meghnad Saha pointed him to a derivation by Wolfgang Pauli that relied on a conjectured probability which "appeared crazy at the time."

Bose wrote that Einstein's own attempt was remarkably elegant but not fully justified logically. His aim, as he put it to Einstein, was to deduce the coefficient in Planck's law independently of classical electrodynamics. He succeeded, and the result was, in his words, "the same as Planck's formula."

How to count a crowd of identical things

The heart of Bose's argument is a way of counting. He treated radiation as a gas of light quanta, what we now call photons, and asked how many distinct arrangements of those quanta are possible. The first factor in Planck's law, up to a factor of 2, then emerged as the number of irreducible cells in photon phase space, which equals the number of photon quantum states. In his letter he assumed that "the ultimate elementary regions in Phase space have the content h³."

The decisive step was what he refused to count. Photon states are distinguished only by how many photons sit in each one. Photons are not labelled individuals that can be swapped to give a new case.

A small illustration, which is mine and not Bose's, shows why this matters. Put two coins in a drawer and ask how many outcomes there are for heads and tails. If the coins carry names, there are four: heads-heads, heads-tails, tails-heads, tails-tails. If the coins are perfectly indistinguishable, "heads-tails" and "tails-heads" are the same situation, and there are only three. Bose applied that second kind of counting to light, and it reproduced Planck's law without borrowing from classical electrodynamics.

There was one loose end. His derivation gave the right answer only after a factor of 2 was supplied. Einstein attributed it to the two polarisation states of light. In a postcard he judged that Bose was the first to derive the factor quantum-theoretically, though "not wholly rigorously" because of that factor.

The physicist Partha Ghose later recalled a conversation with Bose in the 1970s. Bose told him that his derivation had first produced 4πν²/c³ where Planck's law needs 8πν²/c³, and that he had suggested photon spin could supply the missing factor of 2. Einstein, in Ghose's retelling, crossed it out. "The old man crossed it out!" Bose said, and Einstein reportedly asked, "What on earth can the polarization of a particle mean?" Photon spin was in fact studied in 1931, when C. V. Raman and S. Bhagavantam published a paper on it. This is a recollection passed on decades later, so it is best read as a story Bose told rather than a documented record.

Einstein sees the larger idea

What made Einstein's endorsement more than politeness was what he did with the method. Bose's way of counting implied that light behaves in a manner that mattered beyond light. Einstein recognised that the same statistics could apply to particles of matter whose number is conserved. In January 1925 he published "Quantentheorie des einatomigen idealen Gases" in the proceedings of the Prussian Academy of Sciences. It predicted what became known as Bose–Einstein condensation, in which a gas of such particles cooled enough would pile into its lowest energy state, and it connected the idea to superfluidity.

The prediction waited seven decades. Bose–Einstein condensation was achieved experimentally in 1995, and the 2001 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Eric Cornell, Wolfgang Ketterle and Carl Wieman "for the achievement of Bose-Einstein condensation in dilute gases of alkali atoms, and for early fundamental studies of the properties of the condensates." Paul Dirac later coined the word "boson" for particles that obey Bose–Einstein statistics, and "fermion" for the other class, which follow different rules. The name of a Dacca lecturer is now baked into the vocabulary of every physics student.

The quiet aftermath

Einstein's attention changed Bose's life in a practical way. Dacca granted him two years of leave to work in Europe, which began in 1924. He went first to Paris, where he met Paul Langevin and Louis de Broglie, and later travelled to Berlin, where he met Einstein and attended Max Born's course on quantum theory. The meeting with Einstein was not the start of a partnership. Einstein objected to a probability formula Bose proposed for particles in a radiation field at thermal equilibrium, and Bose did not return to the question. The two did not collaborate again.

According to Ghose, Bose found one of Einstein's remarks "sharp and cryptic" and was disappointed by it. In later decades he turned to other work, including a handful of mostly mathematical papers on Einstein's unified field theory in 1953 and 1954. He tried and failed in the 1950s to obtain a US visa to meet Einstein again.

Recognition from India and the wider world arrived slowly. Bose received the Padma Vibhushan in 1954, was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1958, which MacTutor calls his greatest honour, and was made National Professor in 1959. He died in Calcutta on 4 February 1974. The S. N. Bose National Centre for Basic Sciences was established in 1986. There is, as one profile puts it, "some residual discontent" that he never received a Nobel Prize while others won for confirming consequences of his ideas.

What a stranger's counting teaches

The physics lesson is that identity is a fact about the world, not about our bookkeeping. Whether two things can be told apart changes how many ways they can be arranged, and therefore what the world looks like at large scales: lasers, superfluids and the condensates of the 1990s all trace back to that one decision about what to count.

The human lesson is quieter. Bose's first attempt at publication went nowhere. What he had was a precise doubt about a derivation that others had accepted, and the courage to write a short letter to someone far above him. Einstein, for his part, read the paper of a stranger carefully enough to see more in it than its author had, and then spent his own time translating it. Science has, at its best, a habit of attending to the right idea whatever its postal address.

As for credit, Bose seems to have carried it lightly. Ghose recalls him saying: "How does it matter who discovered it? It's been found, hasn't it?" It is a line worth keeping for anyone who has done careful work that someone else later made famous.
