If You Do Not Like a Book, Throw It Away: Perumal Murugan and the Court That Resurrected a Writer

The obituary a living man wrote

On a Monday night in January 2015, a Tamil professor in Namakkal opened Facebook and declared himself dead. In the English rendering Scroll carried the next day, the post began: "Author Perumal Murugan has died. He is no god, so he is not going to resurrect himself." He withdrew his books that were not self-published, said none should be sold again, offered to compensate publishers and told buyers they could burn their copies. He asked not to be invited to literary events. The last line was: "Please leave him alone."

He was 48 at the time, and the novel that had brought this on had been on bookshelves for four years. That detail is worth sitting with. Nothing in the book had changed between 2010 and the winter of 2014. What changed was that a few pages of it, photocopied and handed around a small town, reached people who had never read the whole thing.

I chose this story for today because it sits where my two preoccupations meet: what a novel can do to a community, and what an administration does when the community is angry. The book is a quiet one. The administrative response to it is the louder drama, and it ended in a 158-page judgment that deserves to be read by anyone who has ever sat in a room trying to "restore calm".

A childless couple and a chariot festival

Madhorubagan was published in Tamil by Kalachuvadu in 2010. Anirudhan Vasudevan's English translation, One Part Woman, came out from Penguin India in 2013. The title points to Ardhanarishwara, Shiva in the form that is half woman, the deity of the Tiruchengode temple at the heart of the story and of the author's home territory.

The story is domestic and small. Kali and Ponna are a childless couple in a Gounder farming community, and everyone around them has an opinion about it. Relatives taunt, neighbours whisper, and Ponna is eventually persuaded toward the temple's festival, where, according to the Outlook account of the book, a childless woman on the last night may go with a man of her choice, and any child born is called saami pillai, a god's child. Kali is the book's wounded centre. He loves his wife, he is repelled by what the village expects, and the novel follows the slow damage that silence does to a marriage. It is a book about grief and pride, not about titillation, which is partly why the later fury looks so strange in hindsight.

Murugan was not an outsider to this world. He was born in 1966 in Kootapalli, near Tiruchengode, to a farming family, studied Tamil literature, and took his doctorate at Madras University with a thesis on the writer R. Shanmugasundaram. He taught Tamil at an arts college near Namakkal and had published his first novel, Eru Veyyil, in 1991. Outlook counted 35 published works by 2015, including nine novels, and a regional lexicon of Kongu Tamil. He has said the novel was written in 2010 and set about a hundred years before. The ritual itself is something he reportedly researched. Outlook quoted a temple priest saying childless couples still pray at the shrine, and an activist saying practices of this kind persisted across the state. I cannot adjudicate that history from here, and the court's reasoning, as we will see, did not need it to.

Seventeen days from protest to obituary

Outlook's timeline is the cleanest. On 26 December 2014, fringe groups and the RSS began protesting in Tiruchengode, and the Kongu Vellala Gounder community joined, calling the book's portrayal of Gounders defamatory. By 28 December the protest had become a demand for a ban. Copies were burned, according to the Indian Express report cited on Wikipedia, and, in Murugan's own account, photocopied pages were handed to thousands of people. Scroll reported that after repeated threatening phone calls, police advised him to leave his home in Namakkal.

On 7 January he met community members and agreed to drop references to the novel's real setting. It did not satisfy anyone. Then came 12 January, the day of what the Namakkal administration called a peace committee meeting at the Collectorate. According to Sabrang's account of the judgment, those present included district officials, police, community representatives, Murugan and his lawyer G.R. Swaminathan. Murugan agreed to apologise, although his counsel advised against it. Officials, the court later said, had allowed the groups outside "to have their way by compelling the author to use certain words of apology, which he was not willing to do." That night came the Facebook post.

Mark the structure of this, because it is the real subject. Nobody passed an order banning the book. No court had found it obscene. A room full of people with the authority to keep order had instead concluded that the cheapest way to end a disturbance was to ask the writer to yield. The state was neutral in the sense that a referee who lets one team bring a mob onto the pitch is neutral.

The judgment: a book is not a statue

A petition from the Progressive Writers and Artists Association reached the Madras High Court within days, as Outlook noted. Other petitions, including a plea by the "people of Tiruchengode" seeking criminal proceedings, were heard with it. On 5 July 2016 a Division Bench of Chief Justice Sanjay Kishan Kaul and Justice Pushpa Sathyanarayana dismissed the petitions against the author and the novel. LiveLaw reports that Justice Kaul wrote the 158-page judgment, and that the Bench held the 12 January settlement to have no binding force.

The reasoning repays a slow read. The court sorted nineteen Supreme Court judgments into six themes, among them freedom of expression, obscenity, the state's duty to protect individual rights and the problem of extra-legal village councils. It quoted S. Khushboo v. Kanniammal: "Morality and criminality are far from being coextensive." It said a book "is not to be read like a statue", meaning it cannot be judged by a single passage, and must be taken as a whole. On the prose, Kaul conceded the language could be called "rustic and a little crass" and asked whether that was "by itself fatal." His answer was no.

On the state, the court was blunter than courts usually are: "the State cannot plead its inability to handle the problem of a hostile audience." It then laid down guidelines. In summary, there is a presumption in favour of free speech under Article 19(1)(a) unless a court finds a reasonable restriction under Article 19(2). Law and order duties do not allow officials to compel artists to withdraw. Non-state actors cannot decide what is permissible. Authors under attack must get police protection. Officials need sensitisation, and an expert body from literature and the arts was to be formed within three months.

The lines most people remember are the plainest ones: "If you do not like a book, throw it away. There is no compulsion to read a book." And the last sentence of the judgment, which answers the Facebook post directly: "Let the author be resurrected to what he is best at. Write."

What a resurrected writer did next

Murugan's reaction to the verdict was modest and exact. "The judgment gives me much happiness," he said, and called it something that "comforts a heart that had shrunk itself and had wilted." He said he would get up and write again. He did. After the ruling he published a poetry collection, Kozhaiyin Padalkal, in English Songs of a Coward, Poems of Exile. The Tamil novel Poonachi appeared in 2016, and his later fiction has travelled widely in translation. Pyre, translated by Anirudhan Vasudevan and published by Pushkin Press in 2022, was longlisted for the 2023 International Booker Prize. Two sequels to Madhorubagan, written in 2014 and held back by the protests, reached English readers in December 2018 as A Lonely Harvest and Trial by Silence, according to Wikipedia's account.

There is also a coda that complicates the happy ending. The English translation won a Sahitya Akademi translation prize, but Wikipedia records that the award was stayed by the Madras High Court after a challenge, so the book's quarrel with the public square did not simply end in July 2016.

What the story asks of those of us who administer things

This part is my own reading, not something the sources claim. The story is often told as writers against fanatics, and that is true as far as it goes. But the more useful lesson, for anyone who has worked inside the machinery of the state, is about incentives. A district administration facing a bandh threat has every reason to look for the fastest settlement, and the fastest settlement is almost always the one that costs the weakest, least organised party the most. One author with one lawyer is a smaller problem than a town shut down. The court's insistence that the state cannot plead inability to handle a hostile audience is, in effect, an instruction to pay the higher cost of keeping order without outsourcing the decision to whoever shouts loudest.

It also quietly reframes what "neutral" means. The collectorate probably thought it was being neutral in the room on 12 January. The court's finding was that it had, in practice, let the loudest side set the terms. Neutrality in a dispute where one party can threaten a shutdown and the other can only ask to be read is not the same as standing in the middle.

And the plainest sentence in the judgment, the one about throwing a book away, is a rule about proportionality that works outside courts. You have a remedy that costs you nothing and harms no one: close the book. The remedy that costs someone else their name, their job or their home is a different kind of act, and it needs a different kind of justification.

If you want to read the novel before forming a view, and I think you should, One Part Woman is the place to start. Read it before or after the judgment, and ask yourself how the whole book compares with the few photocopied pages that set off the protests.
