The Librarian and the Lost Rulebook: Shamasastry, the Arthashastra and a State That Wrote Down How to Tax

In 1905, in a library in Mysore, a librarian pulled a bundle of palm leaves out of a heap and recognised a book that scholars had assumed was gone. He had no way of knowing it, but he was holding a foundational manual of Indian statecraft, and, for anyone who has ever worked in tax administration, a text that argues about revenue, evasion and honest valuation with startling familiarity.

The librarian was Rudrapatna Shamasastry. The book was the Arthashastra. This is the story of how a text went missing, how it was found by an official who spent his days among manuscripts, and why one chapter on customs duty still repays a close read.

A heap of manuscripts

The Government Oriental Library in Mysore was started in 1891 under Maharaja Chamarajendra Wadiyar X and housed in Jubilee Hall, which had been built in 1887, according to the Oriental Research Institute's history. It sat inside the Department of Education until 1916, when it passed to the newly created University of Mysore, and it was renamed the Oriental Research Institute in 1943. Today it holds more than 45,000 palm-leaf manuscript bundles.

The Arthashastra arrived in the way most manuscripts reach libraries: as a gift. A pandit from Tanjore district handed over several manuscripts, and one of them was a Sanskrit palm-leaf copy of the text. Accounts differ on small details. The Wikipedia entry on Shamasastry says he found it "among a heap of manuscripts" in 1905, and the entry on the Arthashastra describes the donor as a Tamil Brahmin from Thanjavur and the library as headed by Benjamin Lewis Rice. A Swarajya essay places the discovery in 1904. I have used 1905 here, and I could not find the donor's name in any of these pages.

What is not in dispute is who recognised it. Shamasastry, born in 1868 in Rudrapatna in Karnataka and a graduate of Madras University (BA, 1889), had risen to be the library's librarian. He identified the manuscript as the Arthashastra, a work that colonial-era scholars had considered lost. From 1905 to 1909 he published English translations in instalments in the Indian Antiquary and the Mysore Review. The Sanskrit text followed in 1909, and a bound English translation in 1915.

There is a lesson in that sequence for anyone who has handled records. The find was made by a person whose job was custody, not discovery. A manuscript sitting in a heap became a landmark because someone with the training to read it looked closely.

Why a lost book mattered

Among the sources for the Mauryan age were the Indika of Megasthenes and the inscriptions of Ashoka. Here was something different, a hard-headed manual on how a king should run a treasury, an army, a spy service, a court and a market. The Swarajya essay argues that the text "created a storm for all the wrong orientalist reasons", because it cut against the colonial assumption of an uncivilised past, much as the Indus Valley discoveries had unsettled the idea that Indian history began with Alexander. That is one writer's reading.

The book is large: 15 books, 150 chapters and 180 topics, with Book 2, "On the Activities of Superintendents", the longest at 1,285 sentences.

Superintendents are the heart of the book. There is a Superintendent of Forest Produce for each forest zone, and a Superintendent of Tolls, among many others. The state it describes is not a king on a horse but a bureaucracy with job descriptions.

Who wrote it, and when?

The text names its compiler as Kautilya (or Kautalya), and a verse near the end names Vishnugupta. Later tradition identified Kautilya with Chanakya, the counsellor of Chandragupta Maurya, and the identification stuck in popular memory. Modern scholars are far more cautious. Patrick Olivelle, whose 2013 annotated translation is now a standard English edition, notes that the name Chanakya "is completely absent from the text".

The Swarajya essay points out that the text itself never names Chandragupta, the Mauryas or Pataliputra, and treats its names and places as illustrations. So the book does not hand us a Mauryan court diary. What it offers is a theory of how to run a state.

Dating is a live argument. R. P. Kangle's 1965 work is the standard defence of the traditional attribution to the early Mauryan period, around the fourth century BCE. Studies since then have mostly moved the other way. Thomas Trautmann's 1971 statistical analysis of the vocabulary concluded that the book is a composite by several hands, and he provisionally suggested 250 CE for the work in its present shape. Olivelle dates the final redaction to 300 CE or earlier, and the stage he calls the "Sastric Redaction" to between 175 and 300 CE. Rangarajan questioned Trautmann's method, citing uniformity of style.

The safe position is modest: the book grew over time, and its present form is probably later than the Mauryas.

The customs house at the gate

Now to the chapter that speaks most directly to a revenue officer: 2.21, the duties of the Superintendent of Customs. I am relying here on a 2024 article by P. V. Viswanath, which uses Olivelle's translation.

The superintendent sets up the customs house and its flag near the main gate, facing east or north. Traders bring goods to the foot of the flag and announce the quantity and the price. The call goes out: "Who will buy this commodity at this price for this quantity?" After it is proclaimed three times, the goods go to whoever bids.

Then comes the interesting part. The standard duty is one-fifth of the value (2.22.3). If competing bidders push the price above the declared figure, the increase goes to the treasury along with the duty. Misdeclaration is punished hard. A trader who falsely declares quantity or value forfeits the excess or pays eight times the duty. Forged seals draw a penalty of eight times the duty, and unsealed goods twice the duty.

Viswanath's reading is that the auction is not a market tax, as an earlier scholar proposed, but an incentive device. The importer knows the true worth of his goods far better than the official does. If he declares too low a price, someone may snap the goods up at his own figure, and he loses the profit he hoped to hide. If he declares too high, he pays duty on an inflated value. The safest course is to state the true value. In the author's words, the procedure is early evidence of mechanisms for eliciting truthful value reporting, and he compares it with modern anti-evasion rules such as transfer-pricing methods.

Anyone who has argued over the transaction value of imported goods will recognise the problem: the declared price is as old as the toll gate. P. G. Babu and Vikas Kumar read the same auction as an open ascending-bid auction with a reservation price, and suggest parts of 2.21 may be a later gloss. These remain interpretations of a difficult text.

Honey on the tongue

The book is not naive about the people who run the system. Writers discussing the text's views on corruption, including a brief from the Institute for Defence Studies and Analyses, cite Kautilya's comparison of revenue collection to honey or poison on the tip of the tongue: whoever handles it will taste it. The same paper says the text lists forty kinds of embezzlement and compares embezzlers to fish moving under water, whose drinking cannot be seen. I have not seen those lines in the translation myself, so I give them as reported, not as direct quotation.

A system that assumes honest officials is fragile, so the book builds in inspections and spies.

Rates and the ceiling on profit

The rates are worth setting beside modern ones. Shamasastry's translation of Book 1, chapter 13 speaks of "one-sixth of the grains grown and one-tenth of merchandise as sovereign dues", so customs at one-fifth sits above that. The Wikipedia summary adds that land was taxed at about 16.67 percent, with relief in famine, epidemic or war, and that new irrigation projects were exempt for five years.

More striking is Book 4, which, as Babu and Kumar describe it, caps allowable profit at 5 percent on local goods and 10 percent on foreign goods, with heavy fines for profit beyond that and the excess going to the state (4.2.27 to 4.2.30). Whether it was enforced as written, the text cannot tell us.

What the book is, and what it is not

One caution keeps the story honest. The Arthashastra is a normative text: it says what a ruler should do, not what any ruler did, and its customs scheme may never have operated exactly as described.

Even so, it assumes that a state needs revenue, that officials will skim, that traders will understate, and that good design can reduce all three. Every tax department still works on those assumptions.

The man and the manuscript

Shamasastry became principal of the Sri Chamarajendra Samskrita Maha Patashala in Bengaluru in 1912, curator of the Government Mysore Oriental Library from 1918, and later Director of Archaeological Research in Mysore, retiring in 1929. The Maharaja of Mysore gave him the title Arthashastra Visharada. He died in 1944.

He is rarely the name people reach for when they quote Kautilya, yet every modern debate about the book starts from the pages he identified in a heap of palm leaves. Someone has to look after the thing, and someone has to be able to read it.
