The Great Hedge of India

A wall made of thorns

In 1995, a writer named Roy Moxham came across a passing reference, in the memoir of a nineteenth-century British official, to a customs barrier that had once cut across the whole of India. It was a hedge. Not a garden hedge, but a living wall of thorny shrubs, patrolled day and night by thousands of men, running for well over a thousand miles. Almost nobody, it turned out, had heard of it, and it has left almost no trace on the landscape.

He started looking. After research in London and several trips to India, he located, in 1998, a small raised embankment in Etawah district of Uttar Pradesh that may be the last physical trace of the thing. His book on the search, The Great Hedge of India, appeared in the early 2000s. Its central puzzle is a good one for anyone who has ever worked with tax: how does a government end up building a barrier of thorns across a subcontinent, and how does it then forget that it did?

The answer begins, as so many answers in public finance do, with salt.

Why salt was the perfect tax

Everyone needs salt, and almost nobody can do without it, which makes it a dream for a revenue collector. In 1772 Warren Hastings placed salt manufacture in Bengal under an East India Company monopoly, and prices reportedly tripled within a year. The duty was then raised steeply: from 0.3 rupees per maund (about 37 kg) in 1780 to 3.25 rupees by 1788, a rate that stayed in place until 1879. The Bengal tax was the highest in British India. Other British territories taxed salt at less than a third of the Bengal rate.

The arithmetic of the burden is sobering. In the 1780s an average Indian family paid roughly two rupees a year for salt, about two months of a labourer's income. The tax brought in 6,257,470 rupees in 1784 and 1785 alone. By 1877 salt was yielding about 6.3 million pounds sterling, or around 29.1 million rupees, for the colonial treasury.

Here is the problem any economist, or any GST officer, will recognise at once. A tax that differs sharply from one place to the next creates a gradient, and goods flow down gradients. Cheap salt from the lightly taxed regions was worth smuggling into Bengal. The more the Bengal rate exceeded its neighbours' rates, the larger the prize for the smuggler, and the more the state had to spend to stop him. Tax collection had turned into a border-control problem.

Building the line

The response was a customs line, and it grew by accretion. Customs houses appeared in Bengal in 1803. By 1823 posts lined the Ganges and Yamuna from Mirzapur to Allahabad. The barrier was officially named the Inland Customs Line in 1843, and by 1869 it stretched 2,504 miles from the Himalayas to Orissa near the Bay of Bengal. About 1,429 miles of that, along the Sutlej and Yamuna rivers, were the heavily guarded section.

At first the line was dead thorny brush, mainly Indian plum, fixed with stakes. From 1869 Allan Octavian Hume, then the commissioner in charge, began trials with living native shrubs, and a growing hedge began to replace the cut brush. The mix included Indian plum, babool, karonda, euphorbia, prickly pear in drier country and bamboo in places. By 1870 the barrier was said to be nowhere less than eight feet high and four feet thick, and in places twelve feet high and fourteen feet thick. Hume himself described it as, in its most perfect form, "utterly impassable to man or beast." Every mile of dry hedge reportedly took around 250 tons of material to build.

A wall needs a workforce. At its peak, in 1872, the line employed 14,188 men of all ranks. Indian staff earned five rupees a month, better than the three a typical farm labourer made, worked twelve-hour shifts, and were posted away from their home villages. In 1869 alone, the customs men are recorded as walking 18 million miles of patrols.

Figures for the hedge's length differ between sources, and it is worth saying so. The full customs line is usually given as around 2,500 miles, while the continuous living hedge is described variously as more than 1,100 or about 1,200 miles. The line included rivers, guard posts and stretches that were not hedge at all. What no source disputes is the scale of the effort.

The people on both sides of the thorns

The line existed to catch smugglers, and it did. Convictions rose from 2,340 in 1868 to 3,271 in 1873 and 1874 and to 6,077 in 1877 and 1878. The usual penalty for a caught smuggler was a fine of about eight rupees or six weeks in prison, a large sum for a rural household. Smuggling methods adapted, from armed gangs with laden animals to disguised carriers and salt thrown over the hedge by hand.

On the other side of the ledger sat the people who simply did without. Inside the line, average annual salt consumption is estimated at about 8 pounds per person, against up to 16 pounds outside it. According to figures cited from British government sources, consumption fell below the amounts prescribed for English soldiers in India and for prisoners in British jails. This was a tax paid partly in diet, by people too poor to appear in any ledger of complaints.

It is easy to turn the hedge into a symbol, and tempting too, but the men who staffed it were not villains in a morality play. Most of them were Indian: Muslims alone made up about 42 percent of the customs staff, working a tough job for wages that beat the village rate. The system was cruel in its effects and perfectly rational in its logic, and that combination is exactly what makes it worth studying.

The quiet end

The hedge did not fall to a revolt. It fell to a spreadsheet. Sir John Strachey, the Finance Member, reviewed the salt duties, and his recommendations were carried out under Viceroy Lord Lytton. Madras, Bombay and northern India saw the duty raised to 2.5 rupees per maund, while Bengal's was cut to 2.9. With the gap narrowed, smuggling stopped paying, and the line stopped being necessary. It was abandoned on 1 April 1879.

Strachey was not sentimental about what he had overseen. He later called it "a monstrous system, to which it would be almost impossible to find a parallel in any tolerably civilised country." In 1882 Lord Ripon brought the duty down further, to a standard 2 rupees per maund across most of India.

The consequence, by the figures usually cited, was a real improvement in what ordinary people ate. Salt consumption rose by about half between 1868 and 1888 and had doubled by 1911. The duty itself survived the hedge by decades. In 1930 Mohandas Gandhi's march to Dandi made the salt tax the target of the Salt Satyagraha, in which a very large number of Indians were imprisoned, and the tax was finally abolished in October 1946 by the Interim Government under Jawaharlal Nehru.

What a tax border teaches

The lesson most often drawn from the hedge is moral, and it is a fair one. A second lesson is more technical. The hedge was a symptom of a design in which tax rates differed across a single economic space. Whenever that happens, the state ends up policing a boundary that exists only because of its own rate card. The cure Strachey adopted, bringing rates closer together, was in effect an early act of tax harmonisation.

India has lived a modern version of this story. When the Goods and Services Tax came into force on 1 July 2017, 22 states abolished their border check posts, with eight more reported to be in the process of doing so at the time. A trader's truck no longer had to stop at a barrier at every state line to prove that tax had been paid, because a shared tax structure and electronic records took over the job that the posts had done. Analogies between a colonial salt hedge and a modern indirect tax are always imperfect, and the two systems differ in purpose, scale and consent. Still, the underlying economics is recognisable: wherever the tax on the same good varies across a boundary, someone will try to arbitrage it and someone else will be paid to stop him.

The hedge itself was a literal living thing, and it died the way living things do, from neglect. Its route was later converted into roads, and within a few generations a barrier that had occupied thousands of men became a faint bump in a field in Etawah. What stayed was a question, and it is not about thorns at all. When a government finds itself building ever bigger walls to collect a tax, it may be worth asking whether the tax, rather than the wall, is the thing that needs fixing.
