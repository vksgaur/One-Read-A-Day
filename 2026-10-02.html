Premchand and the Ninety-Year Debt

Ninety years ago this week, India's greatest storyteller of the poor died, six months after he told a room full of writers that literature must be a torch-bearer. Six months before that, he had written the bleakest story of his life.

A story written between trains

In April 1935, Premchand was a tired man on his way home. He had spent almost a year in Bombay writing for the film industry, arriving on 31 May 1934 and leaving on 4 April 1935. The script he wrote there, for a film called Mazdoor (The Labourer), showed the conditions of mill workers so vividly that, according to the account on his Wikipedia biography, the film was banned after it reportedly stirred mill workers to protest. He was now travelling back to Banaras, and he broke the journey in Delhi.

It was there, on the campus of Jamia Millia Islamia, that he wrote a short story. An account published in Janata Weekly records that Professor Aqeel, an Urdu teacher at Jamia, asked him for a piece, and notes that Premchand rarely wrote stories to order. The manuscript of the Urdu original is still preserved in the Jamia library. It appeared in the December 1935 issue of the university's magazine Risala, and the Hindi version followed in the magazine Chand in 1936, a few months before he died. One small difference between the two: the son's name, Madho in Urdu, became Madhav in Hindi.

The story was Kafan, "The Shroud." It is, in the words of one reference summary, the author's "best, blackest, and most powerful short story." It is also one of the most argued-over stories in any Indian language, and the argument is a good way to understand why Premchand still matters.

The scene by the fire

The setting is a village on a winter night. Ghisu and his son Madhav, who belong to a leather-working community at the very bottom of the caste order, sit outside their hut roasting stolen potatoes. Inside, Madhav's wife Budhiya is in labour and in agony. Neither man goes in. The summary on Encyclopedia.com says they half-believe she is troubled by some spirit, and they stay by the fire.

By morning she is dead, along with the child. The two men do what poor people in that village have always done: they go door to door, wailing, asking for money for the cremation. The summary records that they gather around five rupees, along with grain and wood from villagers and the landowner.

Then comes the turn that readers cannot forget. They do not buy the shroud. They reason that no one will see the body when it is carried out, and they take the money to the liquor shop, where, as the same summary puts it, they drink and eat "in the lordly manner of tigers enjoying their kill." The money meant for a dead woman's dignity becomes, for one evening, their own indulgence.

Why the story offends, and why it endures

It is easy to read Kafan as a story about two cruel men. Premchand's readers in 1935 would not have found that comfortable, and neither do readers now. The Encyclopedia.com summary reads the story differently: the author's point, it says, is that the men's moral corruption is the product of poverty and a caste system that has left them nothing to lose, rather than some inherent evil. Critics have also noted a Marxist undertone, and the story was adapted for radio and theatre in several Indian languages.

The discomfort runs both ways. Some Dalit critics have argued that Premchand, who was not himself Dalit, portrayed Dalit characters in a way that confirms prejudice. The Wire has carried a piece on the continuing social media criticism of him over exactly this question, and the critic Dharamveer is among the names associated with that line of argument. Defenders, such as those quoted in Forward Press, point out that Premchand wrote a great deal about the oppressed at a time when few Hindi or Urdu writers did. I could not verify the full text of these critiques in the sources I reached, so it is fair to say only this: the debate is real, serious, and unfinished. A story that can still start that argument ninety years later is doing something that most fiction cannot.

What a reader can say with confidence is that Kafan refuses the consolations of sentiment. The poor in it are neither saints nor villains. They have worked out that a world which will not feed them also will not honour their dead, and they act on that grim arithmetic.

The man behind the pen

To understand how a writer arrives at a story like this, it helps to know how he lived. He was born Dhanpat Rai on 31 July 1880 in the village of Lamhi near Benares, and began writing under the name Nawab Rai. His first collection, Soz-e-Watan, held four stories meant to rouse readers toward political freedom. The British collector of Hamirpur, James Samuel Stevenson, ordered a raid on his house, and about five hundred copies were burnt. Sources disagree on the year: some place the publication in 1907 and others in 1908, and some place the raid in 1909 and others in 1910. What they agree on is the consequence. Munshi Daya Narain Nigam, editor of the Urdu magazine Zamana, advised him to take a new name, and Dhanpat Rai became Premchand.

In 1921 he resigned from his teaching post in answer to Gandhi's call. A profile in The Caravan says he then embarked on a life of penury, working as a freelancer who never said no to a writing commitment. He founded the Saraswati Press in Banaras in 1923 and launched the magazine Hans in March 1930. He also edited the magazine Jagaran, which, according to his Wikipedia biography, ran at a loss. By the time of his death he had written roughly three hundred stories, in both Urdu and Hindi, which the Caravan notes fill four volumes of more than three thousand pages.

There is an irony worth sitting with. The writer who gave the rural poor their most unflinching portraits spent his working life as a small publisher and a prolific freelancer, running loss-making magazines and taking every commission. I am not claiming that his own money troubles explain Kafan, and I have found no source that ties the two directly. But it is hard to read about his years of precarious work and then read a story about a shroud fund without feeling that he knew, from the inside, how thin the margin of dignity can be.

April 1936: the torch-bearer speech

Six months before his death, Premchand stood up in Lucknow. On 10 April 1936 the first All-India Progressive Writers' Conference met, and he presided over it. The Indian History Collective, drawing on his address, quotes him saying that literature should be a torch-bearer to all the progressive movements in society, and that a literature which does not rouse a critical spirit in us has no use today. It also records his view that beauty does not lie in hiding the ugly aspects of human life.

These are lofty claims, but Kafan shows how he meant them. The story does not hide the ugly. It puts two men, a dying woman and a tavern in front of the reader and asks what kind of society produces this. The torch is not comforting. It is meant to show you what is there.

The last months

His health, according to a biographical note hosted by the University at Buffalo, had been worn down by lifelong financial struggle. He died on 8 October 1936, of gastric ulcer, dropsy and cirrhosis, as that note records. Godaan, published in 1936, is described as his last completed work. He was fifty-six. The Caravan notes that he was already severely ill when Maxim Gorky died in June 1936, with about four months left to him.

It is a strange symmetry. In April he told writers that literature must carry a light ahead of social movements. In his last year he left behind, in Kafan, a story with no hero and no comfort, and in Godaan a novel that was his last completed work. Both, in different ways, ask the same question: what does a society owe the people it has taught to expect nothing?

What to take from it

Three things seem worth carrying away on the ninetieth anniversary of his death, which falls on 8 October.

First, a writer's moral seriousness is not the same as sentimentality. Premchand could have given Ghisu and Madhav a redemptive moment. He did not, and the story is stronger and harder to dismiss for it.

Second, the debate about who may write about whom is not a modern distraction from his work. It is a continuation of it. Reading Kafan now means holding two thoughts together: that it is a landmark of Indian realism, and that its portrayal of Dalit lives is a legitimate subject of criticism. The best response to both thoughts is to read the story itself, in Urdu or Hindi if you can, and in a good translation if you cannot. Frances Pritchett has made a harmonized translation, reconciling the Urdu and Devanagari versions, which is freely available online.

Third, the facts of his life remind us how much of Indian literature was made on the margins: between trains, in borrowed rooms, for magazines that lost money. The shroud in the title is a small piece of cloth. The story is about how much a society can ask the poor to do without, and how little they may be left to feel about it.

A note on verification: where sources disagreed (the years of Soz-e-Watan's publication and ban) I have said so rather than choose one. The critiques of Kafan are described only as far as the sources I could open allowed.
