The Tax That Counted Windows: Nine Panes, Ten Panes and the Economics of a Threshold

In the market town of Ludlow, on the Shropshire side of the Welsh border, the tax clerks of the 1750s left behind a quiet puzzle. Count the windows of the 493 houses that economists Robert Schwab and Wallace Oates later transcribed from local records, and you would expect the numbers to fall away smoothly, a few small houses with five windows, many with eight or ten, a few grand ones with twenty. Instead the counts jump. About 19 percent of the houses had exactly nine windows. Only about 4 percent had eight, and only about 4 percent had ten.

Nine was not a fashion. Nine was the last number of windows on which a house paid nothing beyond a flat duty. Add a tenth pane of glass and the owner stepped into a new schedule, paying sixpence for every window in the house. Nobody in Ludlow needed to read a treatise on public finance to see it. The tax collector had drawn a line across the town, and the town had arranged itself just beneath it.

How the state came to count glass

The window tax arrived in England in 1696 under William III, in the years when the realm was paying for war and repairing a currency that people had spent decades shaving. The statute that created it, the Taxation (No. 3) Act 1695, names the need for "making good the Deficiency of the clipped Money" in its title. The Treasury needed revenue in a hurry, and it needed a tax that would not provoke the argument an income tax would have provoked. Income tax was thought too intrusive; the Adam Smith Institute notes that it was rejected on exactly those grounds.

There was also a precedent to escape. Charles II's hearth tax of 1662 had required assessors to walk into people's homes and count fireplaces, and Schwab and Oates write that this caused real resentment. Windows were a cleverer proxy. A house of many windows was, on the whole, a house of some wealth, and an assessor could count the windows from the street without knocking on a single door. Cottages were exempt, and so were people excused from church and poor rates because of poverty. As an exercise in administrability, it was a small triumph: a visible base, a cheap audit, a tolerable correlation with ability to pay.

It is worth pausing on that last phrase, because every tax officer knows the trap in it. A proxy is a tax on something the state can see, standing in for something it cannot. The moment the proxy is announced, the thing that can be seen becomes a thing that can be changed.

The town that rearranged its walls

The schedule that Ludlow faced in the 1750s had notches. A house with up to nine windows paid only the flat house duty of 2 shillings. From ten to fourteen windows it paid sixpence for each window; from fifteen to nineteen, ninepence; at twenty or more, a shilling. So the tenth window did not cost sixpence. It cost sixpence for every one of the ten. The same cliff stood at fifteen and at twenty, and the Ludlow data show the matching spikes: about 18 percent of houses with fourteen windows against about 6 percent with thirteen and under 2 percent with fifteen, and another bump at nineteen.

When Parliament lowered the free threshold in 1761, the town moved again. In a second sample of 170 houses from Wiltshire and Hampshire, 28.2 percent had exactly seven windows, against about 5 percent with six and 3 percent with eight. In the earlier sample, only 3 percent had seven. People did not simply live with the tax. They rebuilt their houses around it.

As the rates climbed, the response grew more visible. Pitt raised them in 1784 to offset a cut in the tea duty, and his Triple Assessment Act of 1797 tripled them to help pay for the wars with France. Owners bricked up windows, and the scars are still visible on old terraces. In Scotland, according to the Adam Smith Institute, some householders painted their windows black with white frames, a joke known as Pitt's Pictures. Schwab and Oates report that after the 1797 Act someone chalked on a blocked-up window the line "Lighten our darkness we beseech thee, O Pitt!", as recalled in a Commons debate in 1848.

The English were not alone. France ran its own tax on doors and windows from 1798 to 1926, and mullioned windows, divided into panes, counted as four openings. The trompe-l'oeil windows painted on blank Parisian facades, to keep a building looking symmetrical while dodging the bill, are the descendants of that rule. Britain, Belgium, the Netherlands and Spain each taxed openings at some point, according to a Paris heritage guide I checked, though I verified the dates only for Britain and France.

What the tax really cost

The economist's name for what happened in Ludlow is excess burden, the loss that people suffer by changing their behaviour to avoid a tax, over and above what they pay to the state. A house with nine windows instead of the ten its owner wanted pays no extra tax on that tenth window, so the Exchequer collects nothing. Yet the household is worse off, because it now has a darker room.

Schwab and Oates, in their working paper for the Lincoln Institute of Land Policy, tried to put a number on it. They built a simple model of household choice and fitted it to the Ludlow data. It implied that the demand for windows was not very responsive to price (an elasticity of 0.147), and yet about 30 percent of simulated households still chose to sit at a notch. For those households, the lost welfare equalled about 62 percent of the tax they paid. Across all households, the deadweight loss came to about 13.6 percent of revenue. The authors are careful to call this an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a precise one. They are less careful in their introduction, which says that the window tax "must rank among the very worst taxes in the history of Western Civilization."

There was a second cost, harder to measure. Parliament repealed the tax on 24 July 1851, replacing it with a tax on inhabited houses. The National Archives records that doctors and others had pressed the case that a lack of light was a source of ill health, and Schwab and Oates say Parliament eventually acknowledged the damage done by blocked windows. The old phrase "daylight robbery" is often attached to the tax, but the standard reference account notes that there is no scholarly support for the connection. It is a good story that the evidence does not carry.

Adam Smith gave the window tax a short passage in The Wealth of Nations. The summaries I could open do not agree on its tone: one reads Smith as noting that assessors could count windows from outside, which made the tax relatively inoffensive, while another reads him as saying it was unfair, since a poor rural household might have more windows because land there is cheap. I would rather flag that disagreement than pick a side without the text in front of me.

An Indian echo, and a better way to look for it

Indian tax administration has its own notches, and at least one careful study of how firms respond to them. Keshav Choudhary of the Max Planck Institute for Tax Law and Public Finance and Bhanu Gupta of Ashoka University studied the central excise (CenVAT) registration threshold that applied to manufacturers until 2017: firms with annual revenue of Rs 15 million (Rs 1.5 crore) or more had to register. Their 2024 working paper, "Dynamics of Firm Growth around Tax Thresholds," finds that firms respond by slowing the growth of their reported revenue from far below the line, starting at about Rs 9 million. The slowdown is about 13.7 percentage points against an average manufacturing growth of about 33 percent, and deductible expenses did not slow down in the same way, which the authors read as evidence of evasion rather than a real cut in output.

The finding that deserves a Ludlow-style pause is the comparison with a simple bunching analysis. Counting the pile-up of firms just below the threshold, as Schwab and Oates counted houses with nine windows, would show excess mass starting only near Rs 13 million. The behaviour the authors detect begins much earlier and is much larger. A spike at the cliff, in other words, is the visible part of the response, not the whole of it. The firms that started easing off at Rs 9 million never show up as a spike at all.

The present GST has thresholds of its own. On 10 January 2019, the 32nd GST Council decided on two limits for exemption from registration for suppliers of goods, Rs 40 lakh and Rs 20 lakh, with states choosing between them, operational from 1 April 2019. For service providers the limit stayed at Rs 20 lakh, and Rs 10 lakh in special category states. For goods, the composition scheme limit was raised to Rs 1.5 crore. I have not found a study of bunching or growth-slowing around these GST limits, and the Choudhary and Gupta paper does not examine them, so I will not claim to know what firms did. What the window tax and the CenVAT study together suggest is where one would look, and how: not only for a heap just below the line, but for a flattening of growth well before it.

What a cliff teaches

Three lessons survive the three centuries between Ludlow and today.

First, a base that is easy to see is easy to change. The window tax was admired for being cheap to assess, and that same visibility told every householder exactly which number to avoid. Second, discontinuities do more damage than rates. A smooth tax on windows would have made every household a little darker. The notches made a large share of them sit at one particular number, and made each of them pay a penalty far out of proportion to the extra pane. Third, the costs that matter most are the ones that never appear in the revenue figures. The Exchequer saw no income from the tenth window that was never installed, and no line in any ledger recorded the bricked-up room.

None of this makes thresholds wrong. Small traders cannot bear the compliance load of large ones, and a registration limit is a sensible kindness. It does mean that every limit is a choice about where to put a cliff, and that the choice should be tested against how people actually behave. In 1851 Parliament decided that the cost to health outweighed the revenue. The window tax lasted 155 years, which is a reminder that a bad tax can survive a very long time if its damage is quiet.
