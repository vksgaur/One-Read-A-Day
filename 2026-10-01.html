The Village as Hero: Renu and the Novel That Changed Hindi Fiction

A novel with no hero

Most novels teach us to look for a protagonist. Open the first Hindi novel that decided to do without one and you find a village instead, with its doctor, its quarrelling groups, its temple politics, its festival songs and its first stirrings of party politics. Phanishwar Nath Renu's Maila Anchal, published in 1954, is usually described in a single phrase: the first great regional novel in Hindi. The phrase is accurate, but it hides a more interesting argument. Renu was making a claim about what literature is allowed to sound like.

The argument was a risky one in the Hindi literary world of the early 1950s. Premchand had already given Hindi fiction its moral centre in the village, and Godan remains the benchmark against which later rural novels are measured. Reference accounts of Maila Anchal still call it the most significant Hindi novel after Godan. Yet Renu's village was not Premchand's. The novel is set in a remote corner of north-east Bihar during the Quit India movement, and it is built from the speech, songs, superstitions and quarrels of the people who live there, rather than from a narrator's sympathetic distance.

It is worth asking how one book managed to be attacked as impure and praised as an epic within the same few years. The answer lies in the man who wrote it, the district he wrote about, and the choice he made about language. This piece follows those three threads, and it is careful about what the record supports. Some details of the book's publication history, such as the original publisher, did not appear in the pages I could verify, so I leave them out rather than guess.

The writer who lived in the border country

Phanishwar Nath Renu was born on 4 March 1921 in Aurahi Hingna, a village near Simraha railway station in what was then Purnia district in Bihar (the area now falls under Araria). He studied in Forbesganj and Araria, and then crossed the border into Nepal, where he completed his matriculation at Viratnagar Adarsh Vidyalaya and stayed with the Koirala family. The detail matters. Renu grew up in a landscape where the Indian and Nepali plains blur into each other, where Maithili, Bhojpuri, Bengali, Nepali and Santhali are all within earshot, and where a border is more a rumour than a wall. It is no accident that the English translation of his novel is titled The Soiled Border.

He went on to the Kashi Hindu Vishwavidyalaya, where he completed his Intermediate in Arts in 1942, the year of the Quit India movement. Sources agree that he took part in the freedom struggle, and that in 1950 he joined the revolutionary movement in Nepal against the Rana regime. His first short story, "Batbaba", appeared in 1946. So by the time he sat down to write a novel, he was not an observer who had visited a village for material. He was a political worker, a man who had been shaped by movements, and who had watched ordinary people live through the political upheavals of his time.

That biography explains the novel's texture. A writer who had only read about villages might have reached for the consoling images of Gandhian rural life. Renu had seen the village up close, and he chose not to flatter it.

The Wire's retrospective on the novel's seventieth anniversary makes the point sharply. It argues that Renu depicted villages divided by caste, class and gender hierarchies, in contrast to the Gandhian romanticisation of village communities, and that his characters are "not passive victims but complex, resistant, and rooted in their local cultures." That second phrase is the Wire's own description of Renu's approach, not Renu's words, and it is a good short summary of why the book still reads as modern.

How the book works

The novel is set in a village that the Wire names as the fictional Marygunj in Purnea district. Its centre is not one family or one person. Indira Junghare, who translated it into English in 1991, writes in her introduction that Maila Anchal "is not the story of one person or family, but rather the story of a village and all its residents," with sub-plots running on romantic, political, religious and social levels at once. A young doctor, who the English-language Wikipedia entry says was inspired by Dr. Alakh Niranjan, arrives in the village, and much of the book watches how the place receives him.

What makes the book unusual is its sound. Junghare notes that the language ranges from standard and colloquial Hindi to regional dialects such as Maithili, Bhojpuri, Magahi, Nepali and Bengali, and even the tribal language Santhali. Kathryn Hansen, who wrote her doctoral thesis on Renu, observed that he "has developed a style of Hindi based on rural speech," one that carries the linguistic patterns of uneducated speakers into the literary text.

The Wire gives a small, vivid example. Words like haikot (for high court) and jakshain (for injection) appear as villagers pronounce them. They are not printer's errors. They are the point. A reader who has grown up hearing English words reshaped by the mouths of people who never studied it will recognise the effect at once: a language that has been lived in, rather than a language on display.

The humour follows the same logic. Junghare writes that it comes through the characters' manners of speech, their perceptions of the world and their relationships with each other. Nothing is explained from outside. The reader is expected to listen.

This is the technical meaning of anchalik, the word Hindi criticism uses for the genre. An anchal is a region, and also, in everyday Hindi, the edge of a sari. The title, which translates roughly as the soiled border or soiled edge, plays on both senses. The village is a region, and it is also a cloth that has been trailed through the dust and is not hidden because of it.

Impure Hindi, and a shrewd reply

The reception was loud and divided. According to the Wire, critics accused the novel of containing "impurities," complaining that it lacked "pure" Hindi and stuffed itself with folk songs and dialect that did not belong in literature. Renu's response was to collect those criticisms and use them as an advertisement for the book. It is a rare act of confidence: an author who turns the charge sheet into the blurb.

A study of the novel's critical reception, published in the Inflibnet Hindi e-learning series, records how wide the range of opinion was. On the admiring side, Nalin Vilochan Sharma ranked it among the finest novels, saying he could remove any one work from a list of ten to make room for it. Nemichandra Jain praised its poetic vision, which finds beauty amid suffering. Shivdan Singh Chauhan called it the most artistic and realistic epic of independent India's life.

The objections were just as specific. Ramvilas Sharma, according to the same survey, argued that the method was naturalistic rather than realist, and questioned the author's faith in people and in political action. Devraj Upadhyay objected to the use of dialect words and to what he saw as a lack of philosophical depth in a novel of more than four hundred pages. Another critic, Dr. Indu Prakash, found the descriptions of sexual misconduct in temples and monasteries exaggerated and out of step with village social norms.

It is tempting, with hindsight, to treat the admirers as right and the objectors as stuffy. That would be too easy. Ramvilas Sharma's objection is a serious one: does a book that records everything, sympathetically and without a clear verdict, risk dissolving into atmosphere? The question has not gone away, and readers still divide on it. What has changed is the standing of the choice Renu made. The Hindi novel after him could no longer assume that the educated narrator's idiom was the only literary one. Reference works now credit him as a pioneer of anchalik katha, the regional story, in Hindi.

The writer in public life, and why the book still matters

Renu's later years show that the novel's politics were not borrowed for the occasion. In 1966 his short story "Maare Gaye Gulfam" reached a far larger audience as the film Teesri Kasam, directed by Basu Bhattacharya, produced by the lyricist Shailendra, and starring Raj Kapoor and Waheeda Rehman. His short story "Panchlight" is also among his best-known works. In 1970 he received the Padma Shri. Two years later, in 1972, he contested the Bihar Legislative Assembly election as an independent candidate from Forbesganj. In 1974 he took part in the movement led by Jayaprakash Narayan in Bihar.

Then came the Emergency. Wikipedia records that Renu returned the Padma Shri in protest against the proclamation of the Internal Emergency, and other accounts date this to 1975. Some biographical sources go further and say he was arrested for opposing the government, and that police treatment and imprisonment weakened his health. I have found those details in secondary accounts only, so they should be read as reported rather than as settled. What is firmly recorded is the sequence at the end: the Emergency was lifted on 23 March 1977, and Renu died on 11 April 1977, aged 56.

There is a satisfying consistency here. The man who wrote a novel in which a village speaks in its own voice was also a man who gave back a state honour when he judged that citizens' voices were being silenced. One need not agree with every political choice he made to see the thread running through them.

Why does the book matter in 2026? Partly because the argument about language has become sharper rather than weaker. Every bilingual writer in India knows the pull of the standard register, the pressure to sound correct, educated and neutral. Maila Anchal is a long demonstration that the opposite instinct can produce a masterpiece. A sentence that sounds like a particular person in a particular place can carry more truth than a sentence that sounds like nobody.

It also matters because it resists the two easy ways of writing about rural India. One is nostalgia, in which the village is a lost, harmonious home. The other is pity, in which the villager is only a victim. Renu offers a third option: attention. The villagers argue, gossip, scheme, joke and survive. They are neither saints nor statistics.

For anyone who works with the rules and records of the Indian state, as a tax officer does, the book carries a quieter lesson. Every file describes people who, in the novel's world, would speak in their own idiom and in their own order of priorities. Good administration begins by listening to that idiom before translating it into forms and sections. Renu does not say this. A reader who finishes the book may well think it.

If you want to read it, there are two doors. The Hindi original is the real thing, and the English translation by Indira Junghare, The Soiled Border (1991), is a careful way in. Read either slowly, and out loud where you can, since so much of its effect lives in speech.
