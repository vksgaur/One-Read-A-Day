"Clearly Impossible": Cecilia Payne, Meghnad Saha's Equation and the Recipe of the Stars

In January 1925 a 24-year-old Englishwoman handed in a doctoral thesis at Radcliffe College, in Massachusetts, that said something no astronomer believed: the stars are made almost entirely of hydrogen and helium. A senior astronomer told her the conclusion was wrong, and she softened it. Four years later he reached the same result by a different method, with a brief nod to her. Today the number she was warned off is a textbook fact: the Sun is about 74 per cent hydrogen by mass.

The story is often told as a tale of a woman being overruled by a man, and that is part of it. But it is also a story about how a scientist can be right for the right reasons and still lose an argument, because the argument was never only about evidence. It was about what everybody already knew. And it runs through an Indian physicist, Meghnad Saha, whose equation made her discovery possible.

A lecture, a missing degree and a ticket to Harvard

Cecilia Payne was born on 10 May 1900 in Wendover, Buckinghamshire, and her father died when she was four, according to her Wikipedia biography. She won a scholarship to Newnham College, Cambridge, to study physics and chemistry. Her turn towards astronomy came from a lecture by Arthur Eddington on his expedition to observe the 1919 solar eclipse, the test of Einstein's general relativity. She later wrote that the result was "a complete transformation of my world picture."

Cambridge let her finish her studies but gave her no degree, because it did not award degrees to women until 1948. The Harvard College Observatory library notes that Harlow Shapley, the observatory's director, visited England near the end of her time at Newnham. She asked whether he might want a new research assistant in America. By Wikipedia's account, in 1923 she was introduced to Shapley and a fellowship for women took her across the Atlantic. She was the second person to hold it, after Adelaide Ames.

Harvard had a vast, patient resource waiting for her: a collection of photographed stellar spectra, the rainbow-smeared fingerprints of starlight, which Annie Jump Cannon had sorted into classes. Nobody quite knew why they looked different. Were stars different mixtures of elements, or the same mixture at different temperatures?

The tool from Allahabad

The key arrived from India. Around 1920 Meghnad Saha, a physicist born in 1893 in a village in the Dacca district of Bengal, worked out how heat strips electrons off atoms. The APS history summary says his work related the proportions of atoms in different energy states and the fraction that are ionised to temperature, electron concentration and ionisation energy. The same summary adds that Payne knew his work, and that she met him when he visited Harvard. He went on to be a professor at Allahabad from 1923 to 1938, a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1927 and, late in life, a member of India's Lok Sabha from 1952 until his death in 1956.

Why does ionisation matter for a star's composition? Because a spectral line needs atoms in the right condition to absorb light. Hydrogen's visible lines, the Balmer series, come from atoms already in an excited state. The APS summary reports Payne's calculation that at the Sun's temperature of about 5,700 kelvin only about one in 200 million hydrogen atoms is in that excited state. So a modest line did not mean modest hydrogen. It could mean a huge amount of hydrogen, almost all of it invisible to that particular test. Without a correction for temperature, line strength was easy to mistake for abundance. Saha's equation supplied the correction.

The thesis, and the man who read it

Astrobites describes how Payne turned the equation around. Instead of asking when a line is strongest, she looked at the highest temperature at which a line still appears. Above it, nearly every atom is ionised and the line vanishes. The Saha equation let her work out how small the absorbing fraction is at that point, and by assuming that roughly the same minimum number of atoms is needed to make a line for any element, she could estimate how much of each element there was.

The thesis, titled Stellar Atmospheres, was finished on 1 January 1925. Its result was in two parts. Most elements in stellar atmospheres turned out to be about as abundant as in Earth's crust. Hydrogen and helium were the exceptions, far more abundant than the rest. Physics World says her analysis put hydrogen about a million times above other elements, and helium about a thousand times above them. That was an extraordinary claim against the view of the time. The arXiv review of solar composition notes that Henry Norris Russell himself had written in 1914 that the relative abundance of elements in the universe was like that in Earth's crust.

Russell was director of Princeton Observatory. Physics World reports that he was an outside examiner of her dissertation and that the thesis would not be accepted without his approval. It quotes his reply to her: "It is clearly impossible that hydrogen should be a million times more abundant than the metals." Under his influence, it adds, she wrote in the final text that the derived hydrogen and helium abundances were "almost certainly not real." Harvard's library page gives the same sentence as being on page 188 of the thesis, with the full wording: "The enormous abundance derived for these elements in the stellar atmosphere is almost certainly not real." Astrobites quotes a slightly different phrase, that the values "are regarded as spurious," so the wording in the retellings is not perfectly consistent, though all agree that she disowned her own number in print.

The pressure was institutional as much as scientific. Her doctorate came from Radcliffe because, according to Wikipedia, Harvard did not grant doctorates to women then, and by Physics World's account the thesis could not pass without Russell's approval. She chose the thesis.

Vindication, mostly

In 1929 Russell published his own derivation of solar abundances, using a different method. Physics World says his results agreed closely with hers and that he cited her. The arXiv review calls it the first comprehensive table of solar abundances and says he acknowledged Payne only briefly. Hydrogen and helium were accepted as the dominant elements.

Otto Struve later called her thesis "the most brilliant PhD thesis ever written in astronomy", a line that appears on several of the pages I read. That praise came years after the fact. What Payne herself felt is recorded in her autobiography, as quoted by Physics World: "I was to blame for not having pressed my point." The same article says her daughter, Katherine Haramundanis, wrote that "through her life, she lamented that decision." Physics World also reports that in a 1977 review honouring Russell, Payne called his 1929 paper "epoch-making" without mentioning her own.

What the stars are made of, and who gets to say so

For the physics, the modern picture is plain. The arXiv review of solar composition, using the 2025 abundances of Lodders and colleagues, gives a hydrogen mass fraction of 73.9 per cent and a helium fraction of 24.5 per cent, with everything else together about 1.6 per cent. It cautions that hydrogen cannot be measured directly in the photosphere, so these values come from models and combined analyses. I could not verify from these pages how close Payne's own 1925 figures were, only that the direction of her claim was right and that her magnitudes were what shocked Russell.

Her later career has its own lesson. Wikipedia records that she received the title "Astronomer" at Harvard in 1938, and that Shapley assured the university it did not make her equal to a professor. She was elected a Fellow of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences in 1943. In 1956 she became the first woman promoted to full professor from within Harvard's Faculty of Arts and Sciences, and in 1958 she was appointed Phillips Professor of Astronomy. She died in Cambridge, Massachusetts, on 7 December 1979, aged 79.

There is a way of reading all this as a story about one examiner. That seems too small. Russell was not a fool. He was applying a reasonable rule: when a new calculation contradicts everything known, doubt the calculation first. Often that rule is right. What made it costly here was the setting. The person with the new idea had no standing, and the person with the old one controlled the exit. When a scientific field puts the power of rejection in one pair of hands, a correct outsider has two choices, to fight and fail the degree or to concede and wait for someone else to say it. Payne chose to wait.

She also did something quietly remarkable in the middle of it. She built a method from a physicist on another continent whom she had read and met, and the method carried her to a place her teachers did not expect. Saha's equation was not written to weigh the Sun. Someone had to carry it from one problem to another. Credit in science tends to go to the person who arrives with the answer when the room is ready to hear it. Payne arrived four years early.

If there is a practical lesson, it is for anyone who audits, reviews or examines for a living. The sentence that says a result is "almost certainly not real" deserves a second look when the evidence behind it is strong and the only counterweight is how surprised the reviewer is.
