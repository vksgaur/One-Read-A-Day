The Cow and the Ledger: Debt in Premchand's Godaan

Hori wants a cow. That is the whole ambition of the central character of Premchand's Godaan, and it is enough to ruin him.

In the novel, which appeared in Hindi in 1936, Hori is a poor farmer who buys a cow from the cowherd Bhola for 80 rupees, on credit. From that moment the book stops being a story about an animal and becomes a story about a ledger. The cow is poisoned by Hori's own jealous brother Heera. To keep the police from pursuing Heera, Hori borrows money to pay them off. His son Gobar gets Bhola's daughter Jhunia pregnant, the village panchayat imposes a fine after the Brahmin priest Datadin makes accusations, and Hori borrows again to pay it. Eventually the family must give away their daughter Rupa in marriage for 200 rupees to save the land from auction. Hori's last resolve, to earn back those 200 rupees and finally own a cow, is what works him to death.

This article reads Godaan the way a tax officer or an economist might: as a case study in how a small debt becomes a large one, and how many people take a cut along the way. Premchand died on 8 October 1936, a few months after the book appeared, so next week marks 90 years since the writer left us with his most famous portrait of an Indian farmer. The economics he described has a documented history, and it is worth setting the fiction beside the figures.

The writer and his moment

Premchand was born Dhanpat Rai Srivastava on 31 July 1880 in Lamhi, near Benares, and he came to literature from the schoolroom. He worked as an assistant master at the Normal High School in Gorakhpur from 1916 and later as a deputy inspector of schools, before resigning to write full time. In March 1930 he launched the weekly Hans, a literary and political magazine that, by the account in his biography, never turned a profit. In 1936 he was elected the first president of the Progressive Writers' Association in Lucknow. He died in Benares on 8 October 1936, still in that office.

The timing matters. Godaan was written into a decade in which Indian agriculture had been hit by a collapse in prices. A note on the economic situation in the United Provinces, dated 23 March 1931 and preserved in the Nehru archive, names "the great fall in prices" as the main cause of distress. It records that tenants could not be expected to pay the full rent demand, and that Congress workers in the province estimated the peasants' capacity to pay at roughly half. The provincial Congress committee suggested remissions of 50 per cent for occupancy tenants and 60 per cent for statutory tenants, though it offered those numbers only as a basis for discussion. The note also mentions that a landowner outside the Congress, the Raja of Bara, suspended half the rent on his estate. In other words, even property owners could see the arithmetic.

That is the world Hori lives in. Nobody in the novel needs to explain why he cannot pay. The price of what he grows has fallen, the rent has not, and every rupee he borrows comes with a price of its own.

A loan that keeps finding new reasons

Line up the sequence of Hori's borrowing and a pattern appears that no textbook on credit would call ordinary. Not one of the loans is for productive investment. The first buys a cow, which is at least an asset. The second buys silence from the police. The third pays a fine levied by the village's own council. The fourth, in effect, is the sale of a daughter to protect the land. Each is a payment to an institution: the police, the panchayat, the marriage market, and finally the auction.

That is the novel's quiet economic argument. The credit that ruins Hori is only partly the credit of the moneylender. It is credit needed to stay on the right side of everyone who has power over him. A reader who counts the beneficiaries finds the priest whose accusation triggers the panchayat's fine, the officials who accept the payment, and the lender behind them, and one of the sources I consulted quotes a moneylender character explaining that the poor must offer chickens, milk or butter to the Collector or his deputy. I could not verify that passage against the Hindi text, so I mention it only as a reading of the novel by one commentator, not as evidence.

What I can confirm from the plot is the direction of travel. A wish for one 80-rupee animal ends in a 200-rupee transaction over a daughter and a man's death from overwork. The debt does not grow only because of interest. It grows because each new shock arrives before the last one has been paid off, and because the poor borrower has almost no way to say no to any of it.

The ending is famous. Hori collapses while working, without the cow he wanted. His wife Dhaniya then gives money to a priest as a cow donation, the godaan of the title, in the hope of easing his path after death, according to one reading of the final scene. The ritual gift that a devout family owes at the end of a life becomes the last payment in a ledger that never closed.

What the record says about the real Horis

Premchand was not exaggerating for effect. The historical record of rural debt in colonial India is long, and its numbers are grim.

In 1911, Sir Edward Maclagan estimated total rural indebtedness at about 300 crore rupees. The provincial banking enquiry committees of 1929 and 1930 put agricultural debt at about 900 crore. By 1937, the Central Banking Enquiry Committee's estimate had reached 1,800 crore. Those are the figures as summarised by a study aid on rural indebtedness under British rule, and I have not traced them to the original reports, so they are best read as the commonly cited order of magnitude: debt tripled in the two decades to 1930 and doubled again in the seven years that followed. That second doubling coincides with the price collapse described in the 1931 note above.

The same source records that village moneylenders charged between 24 and 120 per cent a year, with interest compounding for smallholders and tenants. It also lists the legislative attempts to soften the blow: the Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act of 1879, which let courts revise unfair rates; the Punjab Land Alienation Act of 1900, which barred the transfer of land from agricultural castes to moneylenders; the Cooperative Societies Act of 1904, which created credit cooperatives that still covered under 3 per cent of rural credit; and the Usurious Loans Act of 1918, which let courts re-examine unfair interest.

The line most often quoted from this literature comes from Malcolm Darling's 1925 study of the Punjab: the peasant is "born in debt, lives in debt, and dies in debt." Darling wrote about the Punjab, not the United Provinces where Premchand lived, and the quotation is passed around in that shortened form. But the sentence could serve as a summary of Hori's life, and of the novel that follows it to its end.

Economists sometimes describe this as a debt trap: a borrower whose income cannot cover both consumption and interest keeps rolling over the balance. What the novel adds is a point the statistics rarely capture. Hori's borrowing is driven as much by social obligation as by ordinary want. The fine, the police payment and the marriage all belong to the same category, expenses imposed by the surrounding order, and Premchand shows them arriving as a chain that no household budget could survive.

Ninety years on: what has changed and what has not

The most recent large survey of rural finance suggests that the story has changed shape, though not vanished. The second All India Rural Financial Inclusion Survey by NABARD, covering 2021-22, found that 52.0 per cent of rural households were indebted, up from 47.4 per cent in 2016-17. Among agricultural households, the share that borrowed from institutional sources only rose from 60.5 per cent to 75.5 per cent over the same period. Average monthly household income rose from Rs 8,059 to Rs 12,698, but average monthly expenditure rose from Rs 6,646 to Rs 11,262, so the gap between what comes in and what goes out narrowed.

Two readings of these numbers are fair, and they do not cancel each other. The first is hopeful. A farmer who borrows from a bank or a cooperative is exposed to a regulated rate and a written contract, which is a different position from a farmer facing a village lender charging 24 to 120 per cent. Whatever else has failed in Hori's world, the institutional share of borrowing has plainly grown. The second reading is cautious. More than half of rural households still carry debt, the share has risen, and spending has climbed faster than income. Whether a loan is a lever or a trap depends on what it pays for, which is the question Premchand asks of every rupee Hori borrows.

That distinction is useful for anyone who works with credit and compliance. A loan used to buy a seed drill or a pump is a different thing from a loan to cover a fine, a bribe or a wedding. The survey figures alone cannot tell us how the balance stands between those uses, and I do not want to stretch them into a claim they do not make. What the novel offers instead is a habit of asking, whenever debt is counted, what it was for and who received the money.

There is also something to say about the form. Economic history tends to record debt as a total, an average or a rate. Godaan records it as a sequence of decisions, each of which looks defensible on the day it is made. Hori does not choose ruin. He chooses to protect his brother, his son's family, his land and his daughter's marriage, one loan at a time. A reader who has sat across a table from a taxpayer, or a borrower, will recognise that pattern.

Premchand gave his most famous character an ambition so small that it barely counts as one. Hori never gets the cow. A ninety-year-old novel, a set of colonial banking enquiries and a modern household survey all point to the same lesson, that the cost of a small dream depends on the system standing between the dreamer and the thing he wants.
