The Schoolteacher Who Found a Number Everything Falls Into: D. R. Kaprekar and 6174

Pick any four digits

Take a four-digit number, say 3524. Arrange its digits from largest to smallest: 5432. Arrange them from smallest to largest: 2345. Subtract the second from the first. You get 3087.

Do the same thing again. Digits of 3087, largest first, give 8730; smallest first give 0378, which is just 378, and 8730 minus 378 is 8352. Once more: 8532 minus 2358 is 6174.

Now try it on 6174 itself. The digits in descending order give 7641, in ascending order 1467, and 7641 minus 1467 is 6174. The number maps to itself. The routine has nowhere further to go.

Start somewhere else and the road changes but the destination does not. Begin with 1234 and you pass through 3087 and 8352 before arriving at 6174. Begin with 8991 and you go 8082, then 8532, then 6174. The only inputs that refuse to cooperate are numbers like 1111 or 7777, where every digit is the same: subtracting a number from itself gives zero, and the routine ends there, at nothing.

This is Kaprekar's routine, and 6174 is Kaprekar's constant. The claim, as stated on the Wikipedia page on the routine, is that any four-digit number with at least two distinct digits reaches 6174 within seven iterations. You do not have to take that on trust. I ran every case through a short program while preparing this article. Of the 9,990 four-digit strings (counting ones with leading zeros, like 0042) that are not made of one repeated digit, every one reaches 6174. Exactly 2,184 of them need the full seven steps, 1,656 need six, 1,518 need five, 1,272 need four, 2,400 need three, 576 need two and 383 need just one. The one remaining case is 6174 itself, which needs none.

There is something faintly uncanny about this. Subtraction scrambles digits. Sorting digits is a blunt operation with no obvious relationship to the number you started with. Yet from nearly ten thousand starting points, the process funnels into a single drain. Mathematicians call such a number a fixed point, a value that a process leaves unchanged. Fixed points are everywhere in serious mathematics, from the solutions of equations to the equilibria of economies. Seeing one pop out of a schoolroom exercise with decimal digits is a small, delightful shock.

The three-digit version has a constant too, 495, found by the same man in the same investigation. And the question of whether other bases or other digit lengths have such constants was not settled until 1981 for base ten, when a paper by Prichett and colleagues showed that 495 and 6174 are the only ones, with a full classification of the general problem arriving as recently as 2024, according to the same Wikipedia page.

The man who found all this was not a professor at a famous university. He was a schoolteacher in a small town in Maharashtra, and for much of his life the mathematical establishment of his own country did not know quite what to do with him.

The man at Devlali

Dattatreya Ramchandra Kaprekar was born on 17 January 1905 in Dahanu, a coastal town about 100 kilometres north of Mumbai. According to the MacTutor biography, his mother died when he was eight, and he was raised by his father, a clerk with a passion for astrology. Astrology needs little deep mathematics but a great deal of patient arithmetic, and MacTutor suggests that his father's absorption in calculation fed the boy's own love of numbers.

He went to secondary school in Thane, where he spent hours on mathematical puzzles, and in 1923 began studies at Fergusson College in Pune. In 1927 he won the Wrangler R. P. Paranjpye Mathematical Prize, given for original mathematical work by a student, and he took his bachelor's degree from the University of Bombay in 1929. Then he did something that tells you where his priorities lay. He did not chase a research post. He became a mathematics teacher at the government school in Devlali, a town near Nashik, and stayed in that world until he retired in 1962. (The sources I used differ by a year on when he started, 1929 or 1930, so I will not pretend to more precision than that.)

He taught differently from most. The Wikipedia article on him records that he cycled between places, tutored private students, and liked to sit by rivers thinking of theorems. MacTutor says he used his own enthusiasm to inspire students and was often invited to lecture at local colleges about his methods.

His own explanation of the obsession is one of the most quoted lines in the history of recreational mathematics. MacTutor gives it as: "A drunkard wants to go on drinking wine to remain in that pleasurable state. The same is the case with me in so far as numbers are concerned."

The harvest of that habit is a family of curiosities that now carry his name or his coinage.

  • Demlo numbers. In 1923, changing trains at the Demlo station on the Bombay to Thane line, he conceived the idea of studying a particular kind of number, and named them after the station. He published on them in 1948. The best-known examples are the Wonderful Demlo numbers, 1, 121, 12321 and so on, which are the squares of 1, 11, 111 and onward.
  • Kaprekar numbers. A number is one if its square can be split into two parts that add back up to the number. 45 squared is 2025, and 20 plus 25 is 45. 297 squared is 88209, and 88 plus 209 is 297. 703 squared is 494209, and 494 plus 209 is 703.
  • Harshad numbers. These are numbers divisible by the sum of their digits, so 18 qualifies (1 plus 8 is 9, and 18 is 2 times 9). The name comes from Sanskrit, harsha for joy and da for giving, so it means roughly joy-giver. They are also called Niven numbers, after Ivan Niven, who presented a paper on them at a 1977 number theory conference. Even Ramanujan's famous taxi-cab number 1729 is one, since it equals 19 times 91 and 1 plus 7 plus 2 plus 9 is 19.
  • Self numbers. Kaprekar called the process "digitaddition": add a number to its own digits to get a new one. A number that no other number generates this way is a self number, such as 20. Because it comes from nowhere, he also called these Swayambhu numbers, a Sanskrit word for self-born, and the English literature sometimes uses the name Devlali numbers.

Notice how much of this is, in the best sense, play. None of it solves an outstanding problem of the age. It is the work of a person who looked at ordinary numbers closely, for decades, and kept noticing things.

Recognition, late and from abroad

The dates around 6174 are worth setting down carefully, because the sources do not fully agree. MacTutor says Kaprekar discovered the constant in 1946, announced it at the Madras Mathematical Conference in 1949, and published it in Scripta Mathematica under the title "Problems involving reversal of digits" (it dates that to 1953). The Wikipedia page on the routine lists his paper "An interesting property of the number 6174" under 1955. The safe reading is that the idea was in his hands for years before the wider world could read about it, and that the exact publication year depends on which paper you count.

What both accounts do agree on is the reception at home. Wikipedia says that Indian mathematicians at first did not take his ideas seriously, and that his results tended to appear in low-level journals or in privately printed pamphlets. MacTutor notes that his work was reviewed in Mathematical Reviews but that he stayed largely obscure. A man with a bachelor's degree, a school job and an unfashionable subject, recreational number theory, was easy to file under hobbyist.

The turn came in March 1975, when Martin Gardner devoted his Mathematical Games column in Scientific American to Kaprekar's work. The same year, Marilyn Burns mentioned the constant in The I Hate Mathematics Book. Gardner had a talent for spotting the people whom the professional world was walking past, and for explaining their discoveries to a general reader in a page or two. Within one year a retired teacher from Devlali had readers across the world.

The recognition did not translate into comfort. MacTutor records that his wife died in 1966, that his pension was not enough to live on, and that he went on giving private lessons in mathematics and science while living very frugally and spending every spare hour with numbers. He died in 1986. His later papers on the numbers that now bear his name appeared in the Journal of Recreational Mathematics in 1980 and 1981, in the last years of his life. (Wikipedia gives Nasik as the place of death and MacTutor gives Devlali, two towns in the same district, so I leave it at the year.)

Why a toy matters

It is easy to say that Kaprekar's constant is only a toy, and in a narrow sense that is true. Nobody builds a bridge with it. But toys of this kind are how mathematics recruits people. A child who subtracts 2345 from 5432 and sees 6174 appear after a few steps has met, without being told, three of the central ideas of the subject: an algorithm, a fixed point, and the surprise that a process can forget where it started.

The ease of the experiment is the point. It needs only paper and a pencil, and it rewards a curious ten-year-old with something that feels like magic and then invites the follow-up question, why. That question is where proofs begin. It took until 1981 for the base ten case to be closed off for good, and until 2024 for the general classification, which tells you the toy had real depth.

There is also a gentler lesson in Kaprekar's life, and I offer it as a reading rather than a fact from the sources. We tend to measure intellectual lives by institutional rank. Kaprekar had almost none. He had a job that paid modestly, a bicycle by the account of his biographers, and a decades-long habit of paying attention. What he left behind is a set of numbers that schoolchildren in many countries still use to learn that mathematics can be playful. In 1923 he got off a train at a station called Demlo and an idea followed him home. Sixty years of noticing turned it into a body of work.

Try it tonight with the last four digits of your phone number, or a date of birth written as four digits. Write down each step. Count how many it takes to reach 6174, and remember that the number of steps is never more than seven.
