While Lucknow Fell, They Played On: Premchand, Ray and a Game of Chess

Two men sit on a mattress beside a ruined mosque on the bank of the Gomti, a chessboard between them, while an army marches into their city. That image, from a Hindi short story first printed in 1924, has become one of the most durable pictures in Indian literature of how a kingdom ends. It is also, I want to argue, a story that has been read too quickly, by Premchand's admirers and by his critics alike.

The story is "Shatranj ke Khilari", and its two heroes, Mirza Sajjad Ali and Mir Raushan Ali, are wealthy men in the Lucknow of Nawab Wajid Ali Shah. According to the scholar Frances Pritchett, whose essay on the story and on Satyajit Ray's film remains the best guide to both, the Hindi version appeared in the Lucknow journal Madhuri in 1924, and an Urdu version followed in a collection before 1928. The setting is the last days of Awadh, which the East India Company annexed in February 1856.

A game that outlasts a kingdom

The plot is simple, which is why it works. The two friends play chess from morning to night. Mirza's wife complains and makes scenes. Mir's wife, according to Pritchett's summary, has a lover of her own, and he turns up posing as a messenger from the nawab to summon Mir for army service so the house will be free of him. Mir leaves, and then comes back. The friends, driven out of their homes by the nuisance of domestic life, move the board to a desolate spot near a ruined mosque across the river.

While they play, British troops enter Lucknow. The nawab is taken into custody. In the translation Pritchett uses, Premchand's narrator remarks that never before could the king of an independent country have been defeated so peacefully, without bloodshed. One player briefly expresses regret; the other suspects the news is a distraction from the game. Then a quarrel breaks out over a point of chess etiquette, insults about ancestry follow, swords are drawn, and both men die.

Notice what Premchand does with the ending. A story that merely mocked idle aristocrats would have let them be caught out, humiliated, perhaps ruined. Instead he gives them a death. Pritchett points out that Premchand, for all his disapproval of vilasita, the culture of pleasure and luxury, concedes something to them: they were vilasi, but they were not cowards. They will not defend the city, yet they will die over a chess move. The tragedy is that courage exists in Lucknow and is spent on the wrong thing.

That is the first reason the story deserves a slower reading. It is not a simple sermon about decadence. It is an indictment with a flicker of grudging respect inside it, and the respect is what makes the indictment hurt.

What the story leaves out

Premchand's image of a city asleep is powerful, but history gives us a busier picture. The case against Awadh had been assembled for years. William Sleeman, the British Resident, toured the kingdom in 1849 and 1850 and reported on misrule. In November 1855, according to one study guide that summarises the standard accounts, the Court of Directors authorised annexation. The pretext was not the Doctrine of Lapse, which Dalhousie had used elsewhere, but alleged misgovernment under an 1801 treaty. Under pressure from the Resident, James Outram, the nawab was asked to hand over his kingdom. He refused to sign. The annexation was proclaimed in February 1856 anyway.

The British verdict on Awadh was not universally accepted even then, and it has not survived modern scrutiny intact. One standard reference sums up the historians' view in a single line: Oudh was neither as bankrupt nor as lawless as the British had said. This matters for reading the story. Premchand wrote in the 1920s, when India's own nationalist movement was looking for explanations of why a subcontinent had been conquered by a company. The tale of an aristocracy too enchanted by its pleasures to defend itself was a convenient explanation, and Premchand, a reformer by temperament, was in no mood to romanticise it.

The man at the centre of the accusation is a more interesting figure than the accusation allows. Wajid Ali Shah, who reigned from 1847 until February 1856, was a serious artist. He is remembered as a patron of Kathak and a composer of thumri and ghazal under the pen name Akhtarpiya, with over forty compositions attributed to him. He founded a troupe of performers called the Parikhana and staged the elaborate dance-dramas known as Rahas, which scholars regard as forerunners of modern Hindustani theatre. He wrote around sixty books in Persian and Urdu. None of that made him a competent commander, and nobody should pretend it did. But a king who cannot fight is not the same as a culture that is empty.

The war that did happen

The most telling correction to the chess-board image comes from what followed. The annexation was not a bloodless footnote. A study of the episode notes that the Summary Revenue Settlement of 1856 targeted the taluqdars, landholders who controlled roughly two-thirds of the villages of Awadh. According to that source, some 21,000 villages were resettled directly with village proprietors, and the taluqdars lost between two-thirds and four-fifths of their estates within months. The same source observes that about a third of the Bengal Army's sepoys came from Awadh, mostly from high-caste farming families whose home villages now faced heavier revenue demands. I would treat these figures as the claims of one secondary source rather than settled arithmetic, but the direction of the argument is widely shared: annexation angered the very people the Company relied on to keep its army running.

When the revolt came in 1857, Lucknow was one of its principal centres. The nawab's wife, Begum Hazrat Mahal, refused exile and led the resistance; she died in Kathmandu in 1879. Wajid Ali Shah himself was sent to Calcutta on a pension that the same source puts at twelve lakh rupees a year. He landed there on 13 May 1856, and spent the rest of his life at Garden Reach and later Metiabruz, where he built the Shahi Imambara, finished in 1864.

The point is not that Premchand got the story wrong. A short story is not a monograph, and the two men on the riverbank are a symbol, not a census. The point is that the symbol, when it hardens into the whole explanation, hides an angrier and more consequential history. Awadh did not simply fall asleep. It was taken, and within about a year it had become one of the main theatres of the 1857 revolt.

Ray's refusal of the climax

In 1977 Satyajit Ray made the story into his only full-length Hindi feature. The film was released on 11 March 1977, with Sanjeev Kumar and Saeed Jaffrey as the players, Amjad Khan as Wajid Ali Shah, Richard Attenborough as General Outram and Amitabh Bachchan as narrator. It won the National Award for Best Feature Film in Hindi and was nominated for the Golden Bear at Berlin.

What Ray did with the ending is the most instructive thing about it. He added the nawab, Outram and the court, none of whom appear in Premchand, and he changed the finish. In the film Mir draws a pistol during the quarrel, it goes off by accident, the men freeze, and the duel is abandoned. They go back to their game, and the British pass on their way into Lucknow. Ray explained the change in a line that Pritchett quotes: the idea of the two friends killing each other was abandoned because he felt it might be taken to symbolise the end of decadence.

Read that sentence twice. Ray is saying that the heroic, tragic death in Premchand's story offers the viewer a tidy moral, a decadent world that burns itself out. He did not want to give his audience that comfort. Pritchett's reading is that Premchand moves toward a psychological climax, while Ray substitutes anticlimax, a sense of weariness and inertia with little hope or sincerity anywhere. For Ray, history does not end in a flourish. It ends with people carrying on in small ways while the large thing happens elsewhere.

Ray's portrait of the nawab is also gentler than the traditional one. He told The Illustrated Weekly of India that his portrayal rested on the best historical evidence, and one critic notes that he depicted a patron of the arts who was also a poet, songwriter and dancer, complex and contradictory rather than merely effeminate. Even so, Pritchett notes, the film shows the king petulantly rejecting armed resistance and handing over his crown, theatrically, to an uncomfortable Outram. Ray keeps the failure and removes the contempt.

Why the story still matters

There is a way of reading Premchand that makes it a story about other people: idle nobles, a vanished world, a lesson for the industrious. And there is a way of reading Ray that makes it a story about nobody in particular, a shrug at history. Neither is quite right.

What I take from the pair of them is a question about attention. The two friends are not stupid. They are absorbed, in the way anyone can be absorbed in something that is small, absorbing and within their control, while something large and uncontrollable approaches. Most of us have a chessboard. For some it is a feed, for others a promotion, a house, an argument about a trivial point. The story's quiet horror is that the board is real and the stakes on it feel real, so the distraction never announces itself as one.

There is also a lesson for how we read history. The tale of Awadh as a kingdom that sleepwalked into annexation was useful to the British, who needed a justification, and it was useful to later Indian writers, who needed an explanation. The evidence points the other way in several places: a contested pretext, a ruler who refused to sign, a settlement that angered landholders and soldiers alike, and a revolt in which Lucknow's court played a leading part. A good story can be true as a moral parable and misleading as a history, and the honest reader holds both.

Martin Scorsese, as quoted on the film's reference page, praised Ray for capturing what living through a historical transformation actually feels like. I think that is the right test. Most people who lived through 1856 did not know what they were living through. They played, quarrelled, ran their households, wrote their verses. Premchand's genius was to put that ordinary blindness on a riverbank. Ray's was to refuse to let us feel superior to it.
