Twenty-One Elements and One Survivor: Primo Levi's The Periodic Table

Three nights under a blanket

At Auschwitz, in a laboratory of the I.G. Farben rubber works, Primo Levi took a handful of small grey cylinders from an unlabelled jar. His friend Alberto cut one with a knife, scraped it, and watched it throw sparks. Ferrocerium, he concluded: the alloy used for flints in cigarette lighters. Alberto had seen cylinders like it fitted to the tips of oxyacetylene torches.

The two men then pared the cylinders down to the size of a lighter flint, using a metal plate with a round hole as a gauge. They worked at night, under the blankets, by feel, with knives. It took three nights. Lighter makers in the camp's clandestine trade bought the flints. Alberto reckoned one flint was worth a ration of bread, which Levi counted as a day of life. By Levi's own estimate, at least 40 cylinders gave about 120 flints, or roughly two months of life for each of them. The risk was real: a fire in the barracks, or the punishment for lighting a match there, which was hanging. Nothing was discovered.

This is the chapter called "Cerium", and it is the reason I think The Periodic Table deserves readers who have never liked chemistry. Levi writes that in the end the cerium would have liberated them. Asked why the episode was missing from If This Is a Man, he called it a tale of victory, set against that book's canvas of defeats. That account comes from an Aeon essay quoting the writer Angier, and it is worth holding on to: a book the Royal Institution crowned as science writing is, among other things, a book about a man who survived because he understood what a grey metal does when you scrape it.

The book appeared in Italy in 1975 as Il sistema periodico, published by Einaudi. It has 21 stories, each titled after a chemical element and tied to it in some way. The English edition, translated by Raymond Rosenthal, came from Schocken Books in 1984. In 2006 the Royal Institution in London voted it the best science book ever written. It is a strange winner. Several chapters are barely about science, two or three are fables, and one is a letter exchange with a man who worked at Auschwitz. What unites them is a way of looking at the world, and that is what the rest of this piece is about.

A chemist before he was a witness

Primo Levi was born in Turin on 31 July 1919 and graduated in chemistry from the University of Turin in 1941. His degree certificate carried the remark "of Jewish race", a consequence of the 1938 racial laws, and it blocked his path to a settled job. In 1942 he worked at a Milan laboratory of a Swiss pharmaceutical company, researching an oral diabetes drug, until the factory was bombed.

After the September 1943 armistice he and companions tried to join a partisan group in the Alpine foothills. The fascist militia arrested them, and when it learned he was Jewish he was sent to the Fossoli camp near Modena. On 11 February 1944 the inmates of Fossoli were deported to Auschwitz. In mid-November 1944 Levi's chemistry earned him a place as an assistant in the Buna laboratory, which was meant to produce synthetic rubber. That posting spared him outdoor labour. Shortly before liberation he caught scarlet fever and was placed in the camp sanatorium, which kept him off the January 1945 death march. The Soviet army liberated the camp on 27 January 1945, and he reached Turin on 19 October 1945.

His first book, If This Is a Man, was rejected by Einaudi and published in November 1947 by the small house De Silva, in a print run of 2,000 copies. Einaudi issued a revised edition in 1958. After marrying in 1947 Levi took a job at the SIVA paint factory in Turin. He rose to technical director and later general director, resigned the directorship in 1974, stayed on as a consultant, and retired in 1977 to write full time.

So for almost three decades Levi was two people: a survivor whose testimony the world was slowly learning to hear, and a middle-aged industrial chemist who dealt in varnishes, resins and the small humiliations of factory bureaucracy. The Periodic Table is the book where the two finally sit at the same bench. He put the logic plainly in interviews. "I write because I am a chemist," he said. "My trade has provided my raw material, the nucleus to which things join." And, in another: "Chemistry teaches vigilance combined with reason."

That second sentence is the book's working method. It begins, of all places, with a chapter on an inert gas. "Argon" is about Levi's ancestors in the Piedmontese Jewish community, whom he compares to the noble gases, inert in their inner spirits. One uncle, in the summary I read, stayed in bed for over twenty years. Elsewhere he describes how he saw chemistry as a young man: "an indefinite cloud of future potentialities." You can feel the boy in that phrase, before the racial laws, before the train.

Distrust the almost-the-same

The chapters work like experiments, each with a small controlled surprise. In "Potassium", a young Levi assumes the metal will behave like sodium, which it resembles, and an experiment goes wrong. The lesson he draws is to distrust the "Almost-the-Same". In "Zinc", university lab work shows that impure zinc dissolves readily in acid while pure zinc resists it, a fact the chapter turns into a meditation on purity and impurity. In "Nickel", Levi manages to extract nickel from asbestos mine waste, only to realise that his success would have served fascist and Nazi armament.

Then there are the chapters that go sideways. "Chromium" shows how an ingredient added to a varnish formula as a temporary fix survives for years because of bureaucracy. In "Tin", Levi and his friend Emilio fail to make a living from a home laboratory producing stannous chloride. "Arsenic" and "Sulfur" are small dramas of danger narrowly avoided. "Lead" and "Mercury" are fables set in invented pasts. "Nitrogen" tries to turn hen-house droppings into cosmetics and concludes that a substance's origin does not decide its use. A reader expecting a lecture finds instead the texture of a working life: errors, small victories, comic setbacks, and the quiet pleasure of knowing how something behaves.

The moral centre is two chapters near the end. In "Vanadium", Levi corresponds with a former Auschwitz supervisor who asks for forgiveness yet seems to deny the crimes. Levi reflects on how inadequate such a confrontation is. It is a short account of a conversation that cannot succeed, and its restraint is what makes it hard to forget. The Primo Levi Center describes his lifelong effort to understand "the circumstances and forces that turned people into barbarians" and to confront postwar Germans with his testimony. That is vigilance combined with reason applied to the hardest material imaginable.

The final chapter, "Carbon", is the opposite gesture. It follows a single carbon atom from limestone in a kiln, through a falcon's lungs, through air and water, into plants, and finally into Levi himself, where the atom helps him tell its story. After twenty chapters of particular people and particular failures, the book ends by dissolving the author into the cycle of matter. There is humility in that, and also a kind of defiance: the atom outlasts every ideology that tried to sort human beings into categories.

What a reader in a government office can take from it

I read Levi as the patron saint of anyone whose job is to look closely at documents, processes and things that do not quite match. The "Almost-the-Same" is a hazard in every trade. Two invoices that look identical, two firms that look unrelated, two statements that read as consistent: the discipline is to check, then conclude. It is also a modest discipline. Levi's chemist is not a hero. He is competent, occasionally wrong, and quick to admit it, which is exactly why his rare moral judgements carry weight.

The second lesson is about how expertise can protect a person without making them important. Levi's skill earned him a laboratory bench and, with Alberto, two months of bread. It did not make him special in the eyes of the camp, and the book never pretends it did. Most of us will never face anything like that. But the pattern holds in small form: knowing how a thing works gives you a little room to move, and the decent use of it is to share that room with others.

The third is about writing. Levi spent decades in a factory and wrote at the edges of it, and he credited the trade, not the escape from it, with giving him his material. For a bilingual creator with a day job in tax administration, that is encouraging. The ledger and the library are not separate worlds. The book a Royal Institution vote ranked the best science book ever written came from a man who ran a paint factory.

Where to start

Start with "Cerium", then "Potassium" and "Vanadium", and then go back to the beginning. Read "Argon" last if you want, as a prologue to the whole. The book is about 195 pages in at least one edition, which makes it a long evening and a short week. It is an easy book to finish and a hard one to put down afterwards.
