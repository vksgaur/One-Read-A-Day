The Biggest Mistake of His Life: Premchand and the Press That Never Paid

In 1934, two years before he died, Premchand wrote a sentence that no admirer of his novels would want to read twice. "This press is the root cause of all of my problems," he said. "Ten thousand rupees, eleven years of labour, and struggles beyond count. I alienated numerous friends and broke many promises because of this press, and wasted valuable time checking proofs when I could have been reading or writing. It is the biggest mistake of my life."

This Thursday, 8 October, marks ninety years since Premchand died in Banaras, at the age of 56. He was born Dhanpat Rai Srivastava on 31 July 1880 in Lamhi, a village near the same city. We remember him for Godaan and Kafan, for the farmer Hori and the father and son who drink away the money meant for a shroud. We talk much less about the printing press he owned for most of his working life, and about what it cost him. That story is worth telling for its own sake, and because it is a small, precise case study in a question every reader of books eventually wonders about: who actually gets paid when a book is made?

A writer who wanted to be paid

By the early 1920s Premchand was a celebrated name in both Urdu and Hindi. He worked in the Education Department of the United Provinces and earned more on the side, from magazine contributions and book royalties. What he saw of the book trade did not please him. According to an account by the historian Murali Ranganathan, published on The Wire, publishers delayed payments to writers, refused to let them verify sales figures, and translated works without permission.

Then came a turning point. In 1921 Premchand heard Gandhi speak in Gorakhpur and resigned from his government post. Ranganathan's account says he had become convinced that owning a press was essential if a writer was to support himself by writing. The logic is easy to follow. If publishers will not account honestly for sales, become the publisher. If the manuscript, the type and the sale all sit under one roof, the middleman's cut stays with the author.

It is a very modern instinct. Today we would call it vertical integration, or perhaps self-publishing with ambition. Premchand's version of it began with a letter.

The arithmetic of a dream

In June 1922 Premchand wrote to his friend Munshi Daya Narain Nigam about acquiring equipment. He estimated the cost at 5,000 rupees, plus another 1,000 for running expenses. He had already arranged 4,000, and a cousin in Indore had pledged 1,000. He bought second-hand equipment in Benares, ordered Devanagari type from Calcutta, and purchased two printing machines from Woodroff & Co of London.

The press found a home at Madhyameshwar in Benares, in a building that had once housed a Dayanand Anglo-Vedic school. On 22 April 1923 Premchand reported that the machines had arrived. The type and cases took a few more weeks. The press began work on 20 July 1923.

The final bill was 10,500 rupees. Against his opening estimate of 6,000 including working capital, that is an overrun of 75 per cent, a figure that will be familiar to anyone who has watched a government project or a house renovation.

The ownership was divided among four people. Premchand held 40 per cent. His brother Mahtab Rai, his cousin Baldev Lal and his friend Raghupati Sahay held 20 per cent each. Sahay would later be known as the poet Firaq Gorakhpuri. Nigam suggested the name Saraswati Press, from a list of candidates that also included Sahitya Press and Sansar Press.

Premchand had ideas beyond printing for hire. He wanted a lithographic press because the quality of Perso-Arabic type for Urdu was, in his view, inadequate. He planned a Hindi book series modelled on a series called Peeps at Many Lands, priced at ten annas, and expected 400 rupees per volume to cover expenses. On paper it was a coherent plan for a writer who wanted to control his own supply chain.

Why it did not work

The trouble was the unglamorous part. The press never attracted enough commercial printing work. Management passed from hand to hand: Mahtab Rai first, on a salary, then Premchand himself for two years, then Baldev Lal for a year. In August 1928 a professional manager, Pravasilal Varma Malviya, was hired on a salary plus a third of the profits. Benares was crowded with printers, and the income never justified the 10,500 rupees that had gone in.

What strikes me about this record is a quiet detail: Premchand did not even use his own press exclusively for his own books. He kept relying on established publishers. The machine built to free him from publishers had, in effect, become one more business that needed customers.

Meanwhile he took outside work to keep the household afloat, including two years as co-editor of the magazine Madhuri in Lucknow. And in the same stretch of years he wrote Rangbhoomi, which appeared in 1924, and Kayakalp, in 1926. I would not claim the press produced those novels, and nothing in the sources does. But it is fair to say that the novels were written by a man with a second, unrewarding job attached to his name.

His 1934 verdict, quoted at the top, lists the costs in an order that tells you something. Money comes first, ten thousand rupees. Then years, eleven of them. Then friendships, and promises broken. And finally the one that clearly hurt a writer most: hours spent checking proofs that could have gone into reading or writing.

Hans, Bombay and a banned film

The press was also the engine behind his magazines. He launched the literary and political weekly Hans in March 1930, and in its early years Mahatma Gandhi sat on its editorial board. He also edited the magazine Jagaran, which ran at a loss. Neither paper made him money, according to Wikipedia's account of his career, and that is the part to keep in mind: the journals were an act of public service paid for out of a private pocket.

In 1934 he tried something else. Premchand arrived in Bombay on 31 May 1934 and joined Ajanta Cinetone, where he wrote the script for a film called Mazdoor, the story of a textile mill owner who exploits his workers. One account describes the pay as a princely 8,000 rupees, though that source does not say over what period, so I will not treat the figure as more than a flag. The film went to the Censor Board in February 1935. It was banned in Bombay and, after a release elsewhere, banned in Lahore, Delhi and Lucknow within weeks, reportedly over fears that it could damage relations between workers and management.

Premchand left Bombay on 4 April 1935. His letters show a man who had stopped expecting much from the industry: "it is useless to expect any improvement in cinema," he wrote to a friend. "This profession is organized in same way as liquor trade." The wording is from a translation quoted by Live History India, so treat the grammar as theirs rather than his.

Set these episodes side by side, the press, the magazines, the studio, and a pattern appears. Each was an attempt to find a business model that would let a serious writer write seriously. Each ran into the same wall. Literature in Hindi and Urdu in the 1920s and 1930s had readers and admirers, but not a market deep enough to pay for everything the author was also doing to keep it alive.

The last year

And yet 1936 was not a year of retreat. On 10 April, at Lucknow, the Progressive Writers' Association held its first conference, and Premchand presided. The association had grown from the efforts of young writers in London, among them Mulk Raj Anand and Sajjad Zaheer, and Premchand had helped it along by publishing their manifesto in Hans. In his address he called literature a "criticism of life" and argued that it must have social use, saying that literature which does not arouse a critical spirit in us has no use for us today. An English translation by Hans Raj Rahbar appeared in a 1957 volume on his life and work.

He was already unwell. When Maxim Gorky died in June 1936, Premchand was severely ill, about four months from his own death. According to the Caravan magazine, he got up from his sickbed at night to write a tribute, and was too weak to read it at the memorial meeting the next day. That is not the portrait of a man who thought his work was finished.

He died on 8 October 1936 in Banaras, after several days of sickness. Godaan, published that year, is generally regarded as his best novel and was his last completed work. Kafan appeared in the same year.

What the failure left behind

The press, as far as these sources go, never paid back what he put in. The magazine, strangely, did better by outliving its founder. Hans continued under his son Amrit Rai, who also wrote the biography Kalam ka Sipahi that Ranganathan used, until it closed in 1956 for lack of money. Rajendra Yadav revived it in 1986. Among Hindi magazines, that is a remarkable run for something that began as a loss.

There are three things I take from this story, and they are my reading rather than anything the sources assert.

First, ownership is not the same as independence. Premchand wanted to escape the publisher's control and ended up with the publisher's problems: staff, machines, cash flow and competition, none of which he was trained for.

Second, a writer's scarcest asset is attention. His own list of regrets ends with proofreading hours. The money loss was real, but he mourned the time.

Third, the failure and the achievement are not in competition. The man who called the press the biggest mistake of his life is the same man who kept writing, kept editing and kept chairing meetings while ill. It is possible to be wrong about a business and right about a calling.

On the ninetieth anniversary of his death, the better tribute may be to pay attention to the economics of the shelf as carefully as we attend to its contents. His novels already pass the test of time. The question his press raises is whether the system around them ever did.
