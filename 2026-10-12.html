The First Plan to Plan Families: India's 1952 Population Programme and the Cost of a Target

A disagreement at Wardha

In December 1935, an American campaigner for contraception and an Indian leader who had already become a moral authority sat down at an ashram in Wardha and failed to agree. Margaret Sanger wanted Mahatma Gandhi's blessing for birth control. She told him that "no nation can be free until its women have control over the power that is peculiarly theirs." Gandhi held that sex without a desire for children was lust, and that women who wanted no more children "should simply refuse to unite." According to the transcript kept by the Margaret Sanger Papers Project, they did not reach agreement, though he reluctantly agreed to consider the rhythm method and hoped that small families would become a social custom. The transcript was made from shorthand notes and is the only known record of the talks, so the exact wording deserves a little caution.

Sixteen years after that conversation, and four years after Gandhi's death, the country he had helped free did something that few states had attempted. In 1952, India launched a national family planning programme. The Union health ministry has described it as the first such national programme in the world, and a more careful reference source calls it the first state-sponsored family planning programme in the developing world. Either way, it was early, and it was bold.

This is the story of that programme, and it is worth telling for a reason that goes beyond demography. In twenty-four years it travelled from a handful of clinics and a policy of persuasion to a quota-driven sterilisation drive that helped bring down a government. Then it was quietly rebuilt on different principles. Few Indian public policies show so clearly what happens when a worthy goal meets an administrative machine that is rewarded for hitting numbers.

The account that follows is meant to be even-handed. The concern about population pressure in a newly independent, poor country was not foolish. The coercion that followed was not defensible. Both of those things are true, and the interesting part is how one led to the other.

The census, the Plan and Dhanvanthi Rama Rau

The numbers that frightened planners were not subtle. The 1951 census counted 361,088,090 people in India, an increase of about 42 million, or 13.31 per cent, over the 1941 count. The First Five Year Plan, covering 1951 to 1956, treated population as a development problem. It said that "a population policy is, therefore, essential to planning," and it set the objective of reducing the birth rate enough to stabilise the population at a level consistent with the needs of the national economy. Family planning was placed inside the public health programme, as a matter of health and welfare rather than of state command.

The woman most associated with turning that idea into institutions was Dhanvanthi Rama Rau. Born in Hubli in 1893, she was married to Benegal Rama Rau, who later served as Governor of the Reserve Bank of India, and she was the mother of the writer Santha Rama Rau. She had been a founder of the Women's Indian Association, was elected president of the All India Women's Conference in 1946, and in 1949 she founded the Family Planning Association of India. In 1952 she co-founded the International Planned Parenthood Federation and served as its joint president with none other than Margaret Sanger. The government awarded her the Padma Bhushan in 1959. She died in 1987, aged 94.

There is a quiet irony in this. The argument that Sanger and Gandhi had at Wardha, about whether contraception was liberation or licence, was never really settled. It was simply overtaken by the arithmetic. A poor, newly democratic state with a census in its hands decided that it could not wait for a social custom of small families to emerge by persuasion alone, and so it built a programme.

Each successive Plan raised the stakes. The Third Plan, covering 1961 to 1966, spoke of stabilising population growth "at the very centre of planned development." The Fourth, covering 1969 to 1974, declared that "the limitation of family is an essential and inescapable ingredient of development." The Fifth, covering 1974 to 1979, set a target of a birth rate of 25 per thousand. Over this period the preferred methods moved away from the rhythm method that Gandhi had been prepared to consider, towards sterilisation and the intrauterine device. The word that matters in that progression is target. Once a birth rate, and then a count of procedures, became the measure of success, the programme began to behave the way any organisation behaves when it is judged on a number.

Anyone who has worked in a government office will recognise the next step. A target is passed down from the centre to the state, from the state to the district, from the district to the block. At each level someone is responsible for reporting that it has been met. The pressure travels downward far more efficiently than any understanding of what the number was supposed to represent.

When the target became a quota

The breaking point came during the Emergency, which ran from 25 June 1975 to 21 March 1977. In April 1976 a national population policy permitted state legislatures to enact compulsory sterilisation laws. Figures attributed to the Shah Commission of Inquiry, which was appointed in May 1977 to examine the excesses of the period, show how fast the machine moved: about 1.5 million vasectomies in 1975-76, when the programme relied on monetary incentives, and 6.2 million men sterilised in 1976-77, the coercive phase. One account puts the total for that year at more than 8 million procedures, adding about 2.05 million tubectomies, and records 1,774 deaths from botched operations in official statistics.

What the numbers hide is the texture of the pressure. The same account describes sterilisation becoming a condition for things like land allotments, irrigation water, ration cards, medical care and promotions, and says that officials from senior bureaucrats down to policemen were given quotas. The Shah Commission found that vagrants and beggars were driven into sterilisation clinics by Youth Congress workers and the police, and that auto-rickshaw drivers had to show a sterilisation certificate to get their licences renewed. The commission's final report was issued on 6 August 1978, in three volumes totalling 525 pages, and covered what it called "family planning atrocities" alongside prison conditions and torture.

The political verdict arrived at the ballot box. In the March 1977 election, Congress was defeated across northern India. One analysis finds that in the states that had seen the largest increases in sterilisation, Congress lost 141 of 142 seats, having won about 80 per cent of the seats in the previous election. That figure comes from a single analysis and describes a correlation across states, not a controlled experiment, and many things were happening in 1977 besides the sterilisation drive. Still, it is hard to read it as anything other than a rebuke. The Janata government that followed ruled out compulsion and made acceptance of family planning purely voluntary.

The damage to trust is harder to measure than the damage to the party. The cleaner way to see it is in the structure of contraception that India ended up with. After the Emergency, the focus of sterilisation shifted from men to women. In the first National Family Health Survey, in 1992-93, 30.8 per cent of married women relied on female sterilisation and about 4 per cent of couples on male sterilisation. By the time of the fifth survey in 2019-21, female sterilisation had risen to 37.9 per cent and male sterilisation had fallen to 0.3 per cent. Whatever else one concludes, the burden of the state's most permanent method of contraception now falls almost entirely on women.

The same survey asked men what they thought. 35.1 per cent said contraception is "women's business" and that a man should not have to worry about it, and 19.6 per cent said women who use contraception may become promiscuous. Policy history and household attitudes do not map neatly onto each other, and the survey cannot show that one caused the other. But the pattern fits what one might expect after a campaign that left a lasting wariness about male sterilisation, and it shows how long the echo of a bad policy can be.

Rebuilding without targets, and where India stands

The planning documents record the correction in their own dry language. The Eighth Plan, covering 1992 to 1997, still experimented with incentives and disincentives tied to a two-child norm. The Ninth, from 1998 to 2002, moved towards decentralised planning at district level and abolished centrally defined, method-specific targets. The Tenth, from 2002 to 2007, shifted from demographic targets towards helping couples meet their own reproductive goals. The state, in other words, slowly stopped counting procedures and started asking what families wanted.

The result, by the most recent national survey, is striking. India's total fertility rate was 5.7 in 1966. The fifth National Family Health Survey, conducted in 2019-21, put it at 2.0, down from 2.2 in the previous round and just below the replacement level of about 2.1. The contraceptive prevalence rate rose from about 54 per cent to about 67 per cent between the two rounds, and the unmet need for family planning fell from 13 per cent to 9 per cent. In 2022 the Minister of State for Health told reporters that 31 states and Union Territories had reached a total fertility rate of 2.1 or less.

The national average hides a great deal. Five states remained above replacement in the survey: Bihar at 2.98, Meghalaya at 2.91, Uttar Pradesh at 2.35, Jharkhand at 2.26 and Manipur at 2.17. A country with a single fertility rate does not exist. There is a set of regions at very different points on the same curve, and the older arguments about population, which tended to treat India as one block, fit that picture poorly.

How much of the long fall should be credited to the family planning programme, and how much to schooling, falling child mortality and rising incomes, is something demographers debate, and nothing in the sources I used settles it. One programme estimate, from a reference summary, holds that the programme had averted 16.8 crore births by 1996, but such counterfactuals depend heavily on assumptions about what would have happened anyway, and should be read as an estimate, not a measurement.

What the story does show is quieter and, I think, more useful. A government can be right about a problem and wrong about the method. The planners of 1951 were responding to real arithmetic, and the women and men who volunteered at the first clinics were exercising a real choice. The failure began when a goal that belonged to the nation was converted into a number that belonged to an official, and when the people at the end of the chain became inputs to somebody else's report.

Gandhi and Sanger never agreed, and in a sense neither of them won. Gandhi's hope that small families would become a social custom came true, if by a different route from the one he imagined. Sanger's insistence that women should control their own childbearing has been honoured more in the language of policy than in the pattern of who actually undergoes the procedure. When 37.9 per cent of married women are sterilised and 0.3 per cent of couples rely on male sterilisation, the question she put to Gandhi in 1935 is still open in a new form: who, in the end, is being asked to bear the cost of the nation's plan?

Two books for further reading

Non-fiction: Fatal Misconception: The Struggle to Control World Population by Matthew Connelly (Harvard University Press, 2008). Connelly, a Columbia University historian, traces the century-long global effort to control population, in which India's programme and the "massive sterilisations" of the 1970s and 1980s form part of the final act. It places the story above within the wider international politics of the subject.

Fiction: A Fine Balance by Rohinton Mistry (1995). Set in an unnamed Indian city between 1975 and 1984, the novel follows ordinary people through the Emergency. In it, workers at a Family Planning Centre seize street people to fill an unmet quota, and two of the central characters, Ishvar and Omprakash, are among those taken. It won the 1995 Giller Prize and was shortlisted for the Booker Prize in 1996, and it shows at human scale what the quota figures above cannot.
