The Empire That Counted Itself: Todar Mal, Abul Fazl and the Ain-i-Akbari

On 22 August 1602, the man who had counted Akbar's empire was killed on a road in central India. Abul Fazl was riding back from the Deccan to the imperial court when Bir Singh Bundela's men fell on him somewhere between Sarai Vir and Antri, near Narwar. Prince Salim, the future emperor Jahangir, is generally held to have contrived the ambush. Abul Fazl was his father's closest intellectual companion and had opposed Salim's accession.

What he left behind was not a weapon or a palace but a book, and one of the strangest books any government has ever commissioned about itself. The Ain-i-Akbari is the third and final volume of his Akbarnama, the official history of the reign. Where the first two volumes narrate battles and intrigues, the Ain is a ledger. Heinrich Blochmann, who translated part of it in the nineteenth century, called it "the administrative report and statistical return" of Akbar's government as it stood around 1590. It was completed in the 42nd regnal year, which falls in the late 1590s, with a small addition in the 43rd.

Open it and you do not find heroes. You find the cost of running the imperial kitchen, the wages of labourers, the rules for the mint, the ranks and obligations of the mansabdars, the divisions of the fifteen subahs, and, in the third of its five books, the machinery by which the empire turned fields into silver. Book Four turns to Hindu philosophy, law and learning, and Book Five to Akbar's sayings, so the whole is an odd marriage of bureaucratic filing and cultural curiosity. But for a tax officer, Book Three is the heart of it. It is a state explaining how it assessed what it was owed.

That explanation rests on a man who is easy to overlook next to the charismatic Abul Fazl. Raja Todar Mal, Akbar's finance minister, is the person usually credited with the system the Ain describes. To see why it mattered, and why it took two decades of failed attempts to build, we have to go back to the first years of the reign, when the Mughal treasury knew far less than it pretended to.

Twenty years of not knowing

Akbar came to the throne in 1556 as a teenager with an empire that existed mostly on paper. Revenue in those early years was often settled through lump-sum agreements with local zamindars, pargana by pargana. That was convenient, because it required no survey, but it left the centre blind. Nobody in Agra could say with confidence how much land a pargana actually had under the plough, what it grew, or what the harvest was worth.

The cure was information, and it came slowly. In 1567 the imperial diwan, Khwaja Muzaffar Khan, is said to have ordered the hereditary local record-keepers, the qanungos, to send in returns on cultivated and uncultivated area, produce and market prices. The qanungos were, of course, drawn from the very class that benefited from keeping the centre ignorant. A measuring rod had to follow the questionnaire. Around 1573 Akbar standardised the yard. The new unit, the Ilahi gaz, replaced a muddle of local lengths, and the old hempen measuring rope, which stretched and shrank with the weather, gave way to a jarib made of bamboo joined with iron rings. A bigha was defined as 3,600 square Ilahi gaz. (Modern conversions of that figure to acres vary between sources, so it is safer to treat it as a standard unit than to quote an acreage.)

Then came the experiment that nearly sank the whole project. In late 1574 the most productive crown lands between Lahore and Allahabad were divided into blocks, each expected to yield one crore, or ten million, dams of revenue. Each block went to an officer called a karori. Todar Mal was given revenue charge the same year. On paper it was an elegant way to force the state to look at its own land. In practice, karoris squeezed cultivators and embezzled, and the contemporary chronicler Badauni recorded the exactions with evident disgust. Khwaja Shah Mansur, the diwan who pushed the scheme hardest, was executed on a charge of treason in 1581. By 1582 the karori arrangement was wound up. Sources differ by a year or so on several of these dates, which is itself a reminder of how thinly documented the process was.

What survived the failure was the data. Years of karori surveys and qanungo returns had produced registers of plots, crops and prices. The next question was what to do with them.

Ten years, one rate

The answer, proclaimed in 1580 and extended through that decade, is known as the dahsala, from the Persian for "ten years". It is usually credited to Todar Mal. He was no courtier by birth. He had started his career as a writer in the Sur administration of Sher Shah, who had himself tried a measurement-based cash assessment in the early 1540s, and he had a hand in building Rohtas Fort in the Punjab. Sher Shah's example gave the Mughals a template, but Todar Mal's team did something more systematic with it.

The method went in four steps. First, every cultivated plot in a village was measured in Ilahi bighas and written into a register against the cultivator's name. Second, the land was sorted by how often it was sown. Polaj land was cropped every year. Parauti was left fallow for short spells and assessed in the years it was sown. Chachar had lain fallow three to four years and was assessed at reduced rates that rose towards the full rate. Banjar, uncultivated for five years or more, got concessional rates to coax it back into use. Third, for each crop in each pargana, officials took the average yield per bigha across a ten-year base period, 1570-71 to 1579-80. The state's nominal share was one-third of that average produce, which they valued at the ten-year average price. Fourth, the answer was expressed as a cash rate per bigha, to be paid in rupees or dams. A silver rupee was reckoned at 40 dams, and the schedules covered something like eighteen kharif and sixteen rabi crops, from wheat and gram to cotton, indigo, sugarcane and opium.

Anyone who has worked in indirect tax will recognise the logic. Rather than negotiate every year with every cultivator, the state fixed a published schedule, an early ancestor of the idea that a known rate applied to a measured base is fairer and cheaper to administer than discretion. The schedules, or dasturs, applied across a core zone: Lahore, Multan, Delhi, Agra, Awadh, Allahabad and Malwa. Elsewhere the empire did not even try. Bengal, Kashmir, Sindh and the Deccan kept older arrangements such as crop-sharing, visual estimation of the standing crop, or assessments based on past records.

The Ain-i-Akbari preserves these rates, and it also preserves a subtle confession. The figures it gives are the jama, the revenue the state assessed, not the hasil, what was actually collected. Anyone who has watched the gap between demand raised and demand realised in a departmental report will know that the two numbers tell very different stories. One modern statistical reconstruction, by Shireen Moosvi, suggests realised revenue in the core zone ran at roughly three-quarters to four-fifths of the booked figure. I could not verify that estimate against Moosvi's own text during this run, so treat it as indicative rather than settled.

What the schedule could not see

How heavy was the burden? Historians have argued for decades, and the disagreement is instructive. The nominal share was one-third. W. H. Moreland, writing in 1929, thought the real incidence was often well below that. Irfan Habib, in The Agrarian System of Mughal India, first published in 1963, argued the opposite: once extra charges were added, the effective share often approached or exceeded one-half. Moosvi, working from the Ain's own statistics, also arrives at about one-half of the gross surplus in the richest core territory. More recent work, notably by Farhat Hasan, stresses that dasturs were negotiated with local intermediaries rather than simply imposed, so the picture depends on where one looks.

The ten-year average carries a quieter risk. A rate fixed in silver from a decade of prices becomes heavier if prices fall, because the cultivator must sell more grain to find the same rupees. Habib links that asymmetry, along with the gap between assessed and realised revenue and the discretion of jagirdars, to the agrarian distress and zamindar-led unrest of the seventeenth century. The system that made the state legible also made it brittle. A schedule is a photograph, and the field it photographs keeps moving.

The afterlife of the Ain is almost as interesting as the book. When the East India Company became the master of Bengal after 1765, its officials consulted the Mughal dasturs as a benchmark, and the Ain's rates are said to have informed thinking behind the Permanent Settlement of 1793. In 1855 Sir Syed Ahmad Khan finished a scholarly edition of the Ain, and he asked Mirza Ghalib for a verse of praise. What Ghalib sent back, a Persian taqriz dated 1855-56, is a masterpiece of polite irony. In the translation by Shamsur Rahman Faruqi, he says the edition has opened a long-closed ancient book and given it new dress, but he suggests that such labour is beneath Sir Syed's talents, comparing it to freeing a servant who was already free. Most of the poem then turns to the English, whom he praises for their laws, science and steam engines, and says that "none knows to rule a land better than they". A Mughal book of government had become, in the hands of a poet of the fading Mughal court, a mirror for the new rulers.

There is something worth carrying from all this into the present. Abul Fazl and Todar Mal built a state that could describe itself, and in doing so they learned the thing every tax administration eventually learns: the schedule is not the collection. The Ain records what the empire intended to take. It cannot tell us about the harvest that failed, the qanungo who shaded a figure, or the cultivator who sold his bullocks to meet the cash demand. Good administration, then and now, is the discipline of keeping both numbers in view, the assessed and the realised, and being honest about the distance between them.
