Fourteen Ships and a Ledger: How Haiti Was Billed for Its Freedom

Fourteen ships in the harbour

In July 1825 a French naval force dropped anchor off Port-au-Prince. Accounts put it at fourteen warships carrying more than 500 cannons. At its head was Baron de Mackau, sent by King Charles X with a royal ordinance dated 17 April 1825. The country he had come to see had been free for twenty-one years, since 1804, when people who had been enslaved, and who had beaten Napoleon's army, declared the independent state of Haiti.

The ordinance was an unusual piece of paper. It offered French recognition of Haitian independence, which no great power had yet granted, but with conditions attached. Haiti would pay 150 million francs to compensate former French colonists for the property they had lost, in five annual instalments. French ships would also pay import duties at half the usual rate. The historian Marlene Daut, writing about the episode, notes that Mackau's instructions described his mission as "not a negotiation."

President Jean-Pierre Boyer signed on 11 July 1825. He had little real choice. The ships were in the harbour, a blockade was the obvious next step, and Haiti's leaders knew how quickly a former colonial power could try to retake a coastline. The agreement stated that the sum was "destined to indemnify the former colonists." The word that matters there is former. The people who had been bought and sold were now being asked to pay the people who had owned them.

This article is about what happened next, and about an idea that sits at the centre of public finance: a debt is never only a number. It is also a schedule, a lender, a rate, a priority claim on revenue, and a list of things the borrower can no longer afford to do. Haiti's story is an extreme case, which is exactly why it is useful for seeing how the mechanism works.

A sum no budget could carry

To see why the figure was impossible, start with the scale. The 150 million francs were nearly six times Haiti's total annual revenue, according to Daut's account, and one economic history paper estimates the original burden at roughly 280 per cent of Haiti's 1825 GDP. A country can sometimes borrow its way through a debt of that size. It cannot do so quickly, cheaply, or from lenders who see it as a risk.

Haiti did what indebted states do and went to the market. To pay the first instalment it borrowed 30 million francs in Paris, in the form of 30,000 bonds of 1,000 francs each, running for 25 years at a 6 per cent coupon. The bonds were sold to a syndicate led by the banker Jacques Laffitte. The bidding was done under sealed tender, and the winning price, on 4 November 1825, was 80 per cent of face value. Haiti had hoped for 90. In plain terms, Haiti promised to repay 30 million but received about 24 million, and the lenders, who were close to the political circles that had drawn up the ordinance, took the difference at once.

This is the first lesson about debt, and it applies far beyond Haiti. A headline sum tells you little. What matters is the discount at which the money arrives, the interest on it, and who gets paid first when revenue falls short. Haiti was now servicing two obligations at the same time: the indemnity owed to France and the loan taken to pay the indemnity.

Paying the interest and the instalments meant raising taxes on a largely agrarian economy. Accounts of Boyer's government describe harsh taxation to meet the payments, and the money that went to Paris was money that did not go to schools, health care or roads. Payments stopped in 1843, the year Boyer was forced from office, and a moratorium followed. They resumed in 1849, paused again from 1867 to 1869, and then continued.

A treaty of friendship, and a double debt

France returned in 1838. A second expedition, this time with twelve warships, pressed Haiti into a revised agreement that was officially called a Treaty of Friendship. The balance still owed was cut to 60 million francs, which put the total to be paid at about 90 million, but Haiti had to take on further loans to meet even that. On paper it was a concession. In practice it was a renegotiation held in front of a fleet.

What followed is the part of the story that economists and historians now describe as a double debt. Haiti had owed money to the former colonists of France. To pay them, it borrowed from banks, and the banks, rather than the French treasury, became the ones who gained most. A 2022 investigation by The New York Times found that the French government took only about $8.5 million, in today's money, of what Haiti paid, even though the payments were meant for former enslavers and their descendants. Much of the rest flowed to the banks that financed the payments.

In 1874 and 1875 Haiti took large loans from the Crédit Industriel et Commercial, a Paris bank. The Times reported that the bank's profits from Haiti sometimes exceeded Haiti's entire public works budget. Then, in the early twentieth century, the creditors changed hands. A consortium led by the National City Bank of New York, a predecessor of Citigroup, took control of Haiti's national bank in 1910 to 1911. After United States forces occupied the country in July 1915, American financial officers collected Haitian revenue and sent it to New York banks for more than thirty years, the Times found. In one decade, about a quarter of Haiti's revenue went to debts held by that bank and its affiliate, almost five times what the government spent on schools.

A UN News account of the history says that by 1914 more than three quarters of Haiti's national budget was going to repay French banks. Haiti made its final payment on the whole structure of debt in 1947, 122 years after the ships arrived in the harbour.

What it cost, and why nobody agrees

The Times calculated that Haiti paid the equivalent of about $560 million in today's dollars over roughly 64 years, counting the loans taken to make the payments. Other accounts count more than 112 million francs across seven decades. Those are the cash flows. The harder question is what the money would have become had it stayed in Haiti, and here the answer depends almost entirely on one assumption: the rate at which you let it grow.

The economists Kim Oosterlinck, Ugo Panizza, Mitu Gulati and Ann Weidemaier tried to make that assumption explicit. They modelled a debt of 90 million gold francs, the figure after the 1838 revision, and converted it to 2020 dollars under different rates of return.

Assumption about what the money could have earned Value in 2020 US dollars
No return at all about $450 million
Held in gold about $1.6 billion
1% real return about $2.9 billion
Grown at Haiti's real GDP growth rate (about 2.1%) about $22 billion
3% real return about $125 billion

The Times, working with economists, arrived at a range of at least $21 billion to as much as $115 billion in lost growth over time. Thomas Piketty has argued that France should repay at least $28 billion. The authors of the academic paper answer "qualified yes" to whether a legal claim could exist, but call it a long shot, since it would need French consent to the jurisdiction of the International Court of Justice and runs into sovereign immunity.

The range is wide, and that width is informative. The low end treats the payment as a one-off cost that is over. The high end treats it as a lost seed. Which is right depends on whether you believe that an extra few million francs of public money in the 1820s and 1830s would have gone into schools, ports and roads, and whether those would have compounded. The paper's authors think the cost is likely a multiple of Haiti's 2018 GDP, but even they present the table as a set of scenarios, not a single answer.

What a debt takes besides money

There is a plain public-finance point here. A government's revenue is a fixed pie in the short run, and every franc committed to a creditor is a franc not committed to a teacher or an engineer. The Times report gives the comparison that makes this vivid: a quarter of revenue on debt service, five times what was spent on schools. An invoice of this sort does not need to be called a tax to behave like one, and it is a regressive one, because it is paid by a poor population to rich claimants in another country.

The politics have been slow to catch up with the arithmetic. In May 2015, on a visit to Haiti, French President François Hollande said that France needed to "settle the debt," and later clarified that he meant a moral debt. In 2025, at the bicentennial, President Emmanuel Macron announced a joint commission of Haitian and French historians to examine the impact of the indemnity, though reports at the time noted that he stopped short of committing to financial reparations. The commission is a beginning of sorts. It is also a reminder that counting a cost and paying it are different acts.

The ledger and the harbour

It is tempting to read this story as a villain tale, and there are villains in it. But the part that lasts is more structural. The ordinance of 1825 turned a political fact, Haitian freedom, into a financial instrument. Once that instrument existed, it generated its own logic: loans to pay the loan, a revised treaty to reschedule the balance, new banks to hold the new borrowing, and eventually a foreign occupation to protect the lenders. Each step looked, from inside a counting-house, like ordinary finance.

For anyone who works with public revenue, the lesson is uncomfortable and practical. A fiscal obligation should be judged by what it displaces as well as by what it costs. Between 1825 and 1947, Haiti was repeatedly required to put creditors ahead of its own children. The harbour in 1825 had fourteen ships in it. The ledger that followed did the same work for another 122 years, without needing a single cannon.
