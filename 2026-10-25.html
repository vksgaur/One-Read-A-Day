A Voice Over the Wall: Basheer's Mathilukal and the Love Story That Never Meets

A story told like a rumour

"Has any one of you ever heard of a little love story called Walls?" That is how the narrator begins Vaikom Muhammad Basheer's Mathilukal, in the English rendering a reviewer quotes. It is a sly opening. He speaks to us like a man in a tea shop with a tale to sell, and the tale concerns a prison, a high wall, and a woman whose face he will never see.

The story first appeared, according to the Goodreads description of one English edition, in the 1964 Onam special issue of the Malayalam weekly Kaumudi, and it came out as a book in 1965. It is short. That English edition runs to 56 pages. Yet it has stayed in print and in classrooms, and in 1990 Adoor Gopalakrishnan's film version carried it to festival audiences abroad.

What makes it unusual is how it handles the great Indian prison story. By the 1960s, jail was a familiar chapter in the life of any nationalist, and the prison memoir had a settled shape: suffering, defiance, release, glory. Basheer takes the shape and turns it inside out. His narrator is a political prisoner who, when the order for his release finally arrives, does not want it. The reason is a voice on the other side of a wall.

This is the story of how a man who went to jail in the cause of a country's freedom came to feel that freedom was the thing taking his love away.

The man who wrote it had seen the inside of a prison

Basheer was born on 21 January 1908 at Thalayolaparambu, near Vaikom in Travancore, the eldest of six children of a timber merchant. He met Mahatma Gandhi during the Vaikom Satyagraha, became a follower, took to khadi, and left school in the fifth form to join the independence struggle. Travancore was a princely state with no active freedom movement, so he went to Malabar to join the Salt Satyagraha of 1930. His group was arrested before it could break the salt law, and he served three months in Kannur prison. He was freed in March 1931, along with some 600 other prisoners, after the Gandhi-Irwin Pact.

That was only the first prison. Back home he edited a revolutionary journal called Ujjivanam, an arrest warrant followed, and he left Kerala. What came next reads like the biography of three men. Over roughly seven years he travelled across India and beyond, working, by his biographers' account, as a loom fitter, fortune teller, cook, newspaper and fruit seller, accountant, watchman and shepherd. He spent more than five years living with Hindu saints and Sufi mystics in the Himalayas and the Ganges basin. He did menial work in Ajmer, Peshawar, Kashmir and Calcutta before drifting back to Ernakulam in the mid-1930s.

He started writing in earnest, sold his own books door to door, and ran a bookstall in Ernakulam. His first published story, "Ente Thankam", appeared in 1937. Then came the second prison. Arrested at Kottayam in 1941 or 1942, he was sentenced to two years and six months and sent to the Central Jail at Poojappura in Thiruvananthapuram. There he wrote Premalekhanam (1943) while still inside.

So when he later wrote about a prison, Basheer knew the place from both sides of the gate. A commentator in the Indian Cultural Forum notes that most of what he wrote can be traced to specific episodes in his own life, and Mathilukal is a clear case.

What happens at the wall

The plot is simple enough to tell in a paragraph, which is part of its power. A prisoner, Basheer himself, hears a woman's laughter from the women's section next door. The two gradually become acquainted. Her name is Narayani. They cannot see each other, so they invent ways to be present. They toss twigs and small gifts over the wall. He builds a rose garden on his side. At one point a hole is made in the wall, and the warders discover it and close it up.

Then comes a plan. They will meet at the prison hospital on the following Thursday. She has a black mole on her right cheek, and he is bald and will carry a red rose, so they will know each other. On Wednesday, the release order arrives. According to the Indian Cultural Forum's account, his first reaction is the question "But who wants freedom?" As he walks out, he sees a dry twig rising into the air over the wall and can only pray for her.

The film adds its own colour. In Adoor Gopalakrishnan's version, Basheer is first told that political prisoners are to be pardoned and finds that his name is missing from the list, and he sinks into a low mood before the garden and Narayani revive him. These are the director's additions or changes, and he has said so openly, as we will see.

Notice what the wall does. It is a barrier, but it is also the only reason the romance exists. On the outside, a political prisoner and a woman in the neighbouring jail would never speak. Inside, separated by brick, they are as close as two people can be, because nothing else is competing for their attention. The prison, in the story's logic, is the one place where listening becomes possible.

A love story that is also a lesson in reading

Critics have read Mathilukal in several ways. The Indian Cultural Forum essay says that on the surface it criticises corruption and discrimination in prison, but that its central theme is desire. It adds that the story performs a reversal: the prison becomes the free space, and the hero struggles to re-enter ordinary freedom. Reviewing a book that includes the story, Aakriti Mandhwani, writing on the Book Review India site, finds it "simultaneously a play on reading as well as the art of narrating". She argues that it blurs lived experience and fiction, so that the reader begins to doubt where one ends and the other begins. The opening question to the audience, that tea-shop gambit, is part of this. Basheer asks whether we have heard the story before he has properly told it.

He was also a writer who trusted plain speech. The same essay credits him with creating his own idiom within Malayalam, with coinages such as romamatangal, "hair-religions", and with avoiding the big canvas in favour of the depth and intensity of a single narrated event. It sets his compassion for thieves, gamblers and the fallen beside that of Manto. In Mathilukal that compassion shows in what the characters care about. The pleasures that matter are small ones: a rose, a twig, a laugh heard at a distance.

The film that borrowed a voice

Adoor Gopalakrishnan's film, released in 1990, was honoured at Venice in 1989 with the FIPRESCI Prize and the UNICEF Award, and at home won National Film Awards for Best Director, Best Actor (Mammootty), Best Audiography and Best Malayalam Film. Mammootty plays Basheer. Narayani is heard but never shown, and her voice is that of K. P. A. C. Lalitha.

Adoor's own notes explain the attraction. He found the story as gripping on rereading in 1988 as before, and he was drawn to the idea of building a complex character through voice alone, and to a relationship between two people separated by a wall. He added characters and incidents of his own, cut some of Basheer's, and borrowed from other prison stories, aiming, in his words, for "a film faithful to Basheer and yet one that has a life of its own."

There is something pleasing in this chain. A writer invents a woman who exists only as a voice. A director casts an actress to be only a voice. And an audience, in the dark, does what the narrator did, and builds a person from sound.

What the walls leave behind

Basheer received the Sahitya Akademi Fellowship in 1970 and the Padma Shri in 1982. He married late, in his fifties, and lived in Beypore on the edge of Kozhikode, where he died on 5 July 1994, aged 87. The English reader can find Mathilukal in at least three translations: Voices; The Walls by V. Abdulla (Orient Longman, 1976), Walls by Nivedita Menon (Katha, 1996), and a later edition translated by Nizar Ahmad. A reader meeting him for the first time could do worse than start here, because the story shows nearly everything he did well: a first-person voice that sounds like speech, a premise small enough to hold in the hand, and a mercy toward everyone in the frame.

It also leaves a question that no commentary quite closes. Basheer spent years of his life working for a country's release from a foreign state. He wrote a story in which the freed man weeps at the gate. One reading says this is a joke at the expense of patriotic heroics. Another says it is simple honesty: that freedom is worth little if it separates you from the one person who has finally heard you. The text does not choose, and the film, by keeping Narayani a voice, refuses to choose either.

The last image is a twig, rising and falling beyond a wall, and a man outside who cannot reach it. It is easy to remember and hard to forget, which is perhaps the best test a short story can set itself.
