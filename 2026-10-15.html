The Novel Egypt Would Not Print: Naguib Mahfouz and Children of the Alley

On 14 October 1994, an 82-year-old man was stabbed in the neck outside his home in Cairo. He was Naguib Mahfouz, the only Arabic-language writer to have won the Nobel Prize in Literature, and the man who had done more than anyone to make the Cairene lane, the café and the clerk's anxious inner life into world literature. He survived. The nerves in his right arm did not. For the twelve years of life that remained to him he could write for only a few minutes a day.

The knife was the last move in a quarrel that had begun thirty-five years earlier, over a novel that Mahfouz had written, a newspaper had printed in instalments, and a country had then refused to put between covers. The book is Awlad Haratina, known in English as Children of the Alley or, in its first translation, Children of Gebelawi. Its story is worth telling not because of the violence it eventually drew, but because of what it says about how a society handles a book that asks the oldest question there is.

A clerk who wrote like a historian

Mahfouz was born in Cairo on 11 December 1911 and studied philosophy before joining the civil service in 1934. He stayed a bureaucrat for almost four decades, working at Cairo University, the Ministry of Islamic Endowments, the censorship bureau of the arts department, the cinema foundation and the Ministry of Culture, and retired only in 1971. The novels were written in the margins of a government career. The Cairo Trilogy, Palace Walk, Palace of Desire and Sugar Street, follows one merchant family through the decades of Egypt's modern awakening and was completed before the 1952 revolution.

He was, in other words, a realist of the old kind, patient with detail and tender toward ordinary people. Then, after a break from fiction following the 1952 coup, he came back with something stranger. In 1959 the daily Al-Ahram, Egypt's leading newspaper, began serialising Awlad Haratina.

An alley with a house at the end

The novel is set in an imagined Cairo alley of the nineteenth century. At its head stands a great walled house belonging to the patriarch Gabalawi, who owns the alley's endowment and has withdrawn from his children after a quarrel. The tenants below live in poverty, extorted by strongmen, the futuwwat, who collect protection money at the point of a stick.

The book unfolds in five parts. The first four retell, in the key of a street story, the lives of Adam, Satan, Moses, Jesus and Muhammad, under the names Adham, Idris, Gabal, Rifaa and Qasim. Each hero rises from the poor quarter, defeats the strongmen of his day and gives the people a measure of justice. Each time, after his death, new strongmen appear and the old order creeps back. The followers of the three prophets settle in separate parts of the alley, a transparent image of three religions.

The fifth part belongs to Arafa, a young man who arrives with something the earlier heroes lacked: knowledge. He is generally read as a figure of modern science. He wants to learn Gabalawi's secrets and breaks into the house, and the old man dies, though not by Arafa's hand. The chief strongman then blackmails Arafa into serving his own ambitions. Arafa is murdered, and the novel ends with a friend searching a rubbish tip for the book in which Arafa wrote down what he had learned. The Swedish Academy's presentation speech in 1988 described the work as a spiritual history of mankind, and noted that it has 114 chapters, matching the number of suras in the Quran. It also noted that Mahfouz rejected being called a pessimist: the ending, it said, leaves a glimmer of hope.

The obvious reading, and the one that set off the row, is that Gabalawi is God. Mahfouz did not accept this. As the English-language summary of his own comments has it, Gabalawi is "a certain idea of God that men have made." In the alley, that distinction does a great deal of work. A reader who takes the patriarch as God sees blasphemy. A reader who takes him as the idea of God that each generation inherits, revises and fights over sees a novel about human beings and their institutions, and about why the promise of each prophet is so quickly captured by the next strongman.

1959: a serial, a protest and a promise

The objections came as soon as the instalments appeared. Religious figures, some of them linked to Al-Azhar, the great Cairo institution of Islamic learning, protested that a novel with characters standing for God and the prophets was blasphemous. Accounts of what happened next differ in the detail, and it is worth being plain about that. Mohamed Hassanein Heikal, the editor of Al-Ahram, is reported to have consulted President Gamal Abdel Nasser, and the serial ran to its end uncut. The book, however, was not to appear in Egypt.

Mahfouz's own explanation, given decades later, was that he had made an agreement. In an interview quoted by the novelist and critic Laila Lalami in 2006, he said that in 1959 he had agreed with the head of publications not to publish the novel in Egypt, so as not to alienate Al-Azhar, and added: "I made an agreement and will honour it." Others, including the writer Mohamed Salmawy, argued in the same exchange that Al-Azhar had never formally objected and had no authority to ban a book. Mahfouz agreed that it had no such authority. In a 1989 interview with Aramco World he said he would allow Egyptian publication only with Al-Azhar's approval.

The result was a long and odd limbo. The novel circulated in Beirut, where it was printed in 1967, and it was banned in other Arab countries except Lebanon. A scholar can read the history as a story of clerical censorship. A political reader, such as the author of a New Lines essay on the case, sees something else: that the religious row gave the Nasser state a convenient screen, because the novel's real target was less theology than power, and in particular the way each liberator's movement hardens into a new regime of strongmen. Mahfouz himself said in 2001 that the book was "above all a political work, and the people I had in mind understood its meaning."

The prize, the fatwa and the knife

In 1988 the Swedish Academy gave Mahfouz the Nobel Prize in Literature, citing "the forming of an Arabian narrative art that applies to all mankind." His interpreter, Hussein al-Habrouk, later recalled that about ten reporters were waiting at the writer's home on the day of the announcement and knew the news before he did. Mahfouz told Aramco World: "The Arab world also won the Nobel with me." His daughters collected the prize in Stockholm because his health made the journey hard. The prize also ended a quieter punishment: many of his books had been banned in several Arab countries after he backed Sadat's peace treaty with Israel, and the bans lifted after Stockholm.

The attention had a darker side. Radical circles revived the old accusation of apostasy. In 1989, according to the encyclopaedic account on Wikipedia, Sheikh Omar Abdel-Rahman, the spiritual leader of the militant group al-Gamaa al-Islamiyah, condemned the novel and called on its author to repent or be killed. A different source, the National Coalition Against Censorship, notes that the sheikh later maintained that he had not issued a fatwa. What is not in dispute is the outcome. On 14 October 1994 a member of the group stabbed Mahfouz in the neck while he sat in a friend's car on the way to his weekly gathering with friends in downtown Cairo. Sixteen people were later tried by a military court; two were sentenced to death and hanged.

Mahfouz went on living in his flat in Agouza, under constant guard from then on. The man who had once written all morning now managed a few minutes. He died on 30 August 2006, at 94, after complications from two falls.

What the alley still asks

The English-language life of the book is its own small drama. Philip Stewart's translation, Children of Gebelawi, appeared from Heinemann in 1981. The American University in Cairo later commissioned a new one from Peter Theroux after Stewart declined to sell his copyright, and Doubleday published it in 1996 as Children of the Alley. In Egypt, a full edition finally appeared in 2006, with a preface by Ahmad Kamal Abul-Magd. The sources I found differ on the exact timing, so I give only the year.

Why does an old argument about one novel deserve an October morning? Partly because the pattern is so durable. A writer takes an inherited story and asks what happens to it when it passes through human hands. A segment of the audience hears the question as an assault on the story itself. A state, which has its own reasons to dislike the question, shelters behind the offended. Years later someone with a knife, who may know the book only by report, settles the matter in the cruellest possible way. The 1994 attack was in this sense less a literary event than the end of a long chain of other people deciding on Mahfouz's behalf what he had meant.

It also matters because the book is gentler than its reputation. Nothing in it is a sneer. Each prophet in the alley is a sympathetic figure, a poor man who sees that the people around him are being robbed and does something about it. The novel's grief is that goodness cannot be stored: every hero's justice goes sour when the heroes die, and the strongmen return. Its final hope, which the Swedish Academy noticed, is Arafa's notebook in the rubbish heap, the idea that knowledge, if someone bothers to look for it, might do what the miracles did not.

Mahfouz regarded the Cairo Trilogy as the more important work, and in 1989 he told Aramco World so. Read the Trilogy first, if you like, for its warmth and for the exact sound of a Cairo household in the 1910s and 1920s. But the alley is the book that shows what a novel can cost, and what it can still be worth.
