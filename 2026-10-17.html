Nine Hours, Three Parties: Gaya Lal and India's Long Fight Over the Defector

In 1967, an MLA from Hassanpur, in what is now Haryana's Palwal district, gave Indian politics a phrase that has outlived him by decades. Gaya Lal had won his seat as an independent and joined the Congress. Then, by the account on his Wikipedia page, he left it for the United Front, came back to the Congress, and within nine hours went back to the United Front again. The whole sequence is usually described as three switches in a single day or, in other accounts, within about a fortnight.

I should be candid that the sources do not agree on the choreography. One account lists four moves instead of three, and gives different party labels. What they share is the punchline. When Gaya Lal returned to the Congress fold, the Congress leader Rao Birender Singh took him to a press conference in Chandigarh and announced that Gaya Ram was now Aaya Ram. The wording varies from retelling to retelling. The pun, built on a name as common as Ram and on the verbs for coming and going, did not.

It stuck because it described something larger than one man. Haryana had come into existence only on 1 November 1966. Bhagwat Dayal Sharma served as its first chief minister until 23 March 1967, and Rao Birender Singh's term began the next day. The assembly had 81 members. According to an excerpt of a Tribune review of a book on the state's politics, 44 of them changed sides at least once between 1966 and 1968: one did so five times, two did so four times, three did so three times, four did so twice, and 34 did so once. On 2 November 1967 the assembly was dissolved and President's rule was imposed, the same excerpt says, because of the frequent changes of loyalty.

Gaya Lal's own career shows how little the label mattered to him. He contested the 1972 election under the Akhil Bharatiya Arya Sabha, joined Charan Singh's Bharatiya Lok Dal in 1974, and won again in 1977 as a Janata Party candidate. His son, Udai Bhan, later became president of the Haryana Congress. The man who became a punchline was, in his own way, simply following the incentives of his time. The interesting question is what those incentives were, and what it took to change them.

What the Chavan Committee counted

Parliament responded to the churn of 1967 with a committee headed by Y. B. Chavan, set up after a Lok Sabha resolution. It reported in 1968, and the figures in its first paragraph, as quoted by PRS Legislative Research, are striking. Some 542 legislators had changed allegiance between the first general election in 1951 and the fourth in 1967. In the twelve months from February 1967 to March 1968 alone there were 438 defections. Of 210 legislators who defected in seven states after the 1967 election, 116 ended up as ministers in the governments their defection had helped to form.

The committee drew the obvious conclusion, in words worth reading slowly: the lure of office had played a dominant part in decisions to defect, and money had been a factor too. It was not a unanimous mood. The CPI leader Bhupesh Gupta, in a dissent note, pointed out that before 1967 defections had mostly favoured the Congress, and that the Congress itself later lost ground, and legislators, to defection. Setalvad and Daphtary, in their own note, wrote that the lasting solution could only come from political parties adhering to a basic political morality.

Other estimates of the scale vary with the method. Wikipedia's summary of the literature says about 550 of roughly 3,500 members elected to state assemblies in 1967 later defected. A 2003 estimate by B. Venkatesh Kumar, cited by the Association for Democratic Reforms, puts it at almost half of the roughly 4,000 legislators elected between 1967 and 1971. These are not the same measurement, so I would treat them as indicators of scale rather than as competing facts.

The committee's report produced a first anti-defection bill under Indira Gandhi's government. It went to a Joint Select Committee and lapsed when later elections voided pending proposals. The country then waited until 1985. Rajiv Gandhi's government introduced the Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Bill in the Lok Sabha on 24 January 1985. The Lok Sabha passed it on 30 January, the Rajya Sabha on 31 January, and the President gave assent on 15 February. Sources differ on whether it took effect that day or on 18 March 1985.

The amendment inserted the Tenth Schedule and wrote the term political party into the Constitution for the first time. A legislator is disqualified for voluntarily giving up party membership, for voting or abstaining against the party's direction, or for joining another party after being elected as an independent. The presiding officer, the Speaker or Chairman, decides.

Escape hatches and an umpire problem

The 1985 law was built with two exits. Paragraph 3 let one-third of a party's legislators break away without penalty, and paragraph 4 protected a merger approved by two-thirds. The first exit was used on a national scale. In 1990, Chandra Shekhar and 61 other parliamentarians changed allegiance together and faced no penalty because the split exemption covered them. In 2003 the Ninety-first Amendment closed that door. The Lok Sabha passed it on 16 December and the Rajya Sabha on 18 December. The President consented on 1 January 2004. It also barred disqualified defectors from ministerial and other remunerative political posts until their term ends, and capped ministries in states and union territories at 15 per cent of the house.

The merger clause survived. The Law Commission's 170th report in 1999 had recommended deleting both the split and merger provisions, warning that the merger clause would cause several complications and unnecessary disputes. PRS cites a more recent case in Goa, where eight of 11 Congress MLAs merged with the BJP, as the kind of episode that keeps the clause in the news.

The deeper design question is who decides. Paragraph 6 made the Speaker the final authority, and paragraph 7 barred the courts. In 1992, in Kihoto Hollohan, the Supreme Court struck down paragraph 7, holding that a Speaker's decision is subject to judicial review. Critics have long asked whether an officer who came to the chair from a party can judge his own former colleagues impartially. The Dinesh Goswami Committee in 1990 and the National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution both recommended taking the Speaker out of this role. PRS also notes that Justices Nariman and Bhat suggested a permanent tribunal. In one Manipur matter, a Speaker is reported to have sat on proceedings against an MLA for more than three years, and the Court recommended that such cases be decided within three months.

That three-month yardstick returned in 2025. In Padi Kaushik Reddy v. State of Telangana, decided on 31 July 2025, a bench of Chief Justice B. R. Gavai and Justice A. G. Masih directed the Telangana Speaker to conclude disqualification proceedings against 10 MLAs of the BRS who were alleged to have defected to the Congress, within three months. The Court said that failing to set a deadline risked repeating the widely criticised situation of "operation successful, patient died." It also said it was for Parliament to consider whether the Speaker-based mechanism serves its purpose.

What followed is a useful case study. According to Deccan Herald, in December 2025 the Speaker, Gaddam Prasad Kumar, dismissed petitions against five of the MLAs, citing a lack of evidence that they had joined the Congress. The MLAs had said they remained BRS members and had met the chief minister only about constituency development. BRS leader K. T. Rama Rao called the ruling a mockery of the Constitution, and the party was expected to go back to the Supreme Court. I have not found a later report, so I cannot say how that ended. The episode shows both halves of the problem: a deadline can force a decision, but it cannot force a particular one.

Two honest readings

The case for the law is the Chavan Committee's own table. If 116 of 210 defectors became ministers in the governments they helped to make, then office was the currency, and a rule that takes away the seat takes away much of the payment. Haryana in 1967 is the image its defenders reach for: an assembly of 81 in which 44 members changed sides at least once, and which ended in dissolution within a year of the election.

The case against comes from Chakshu Roy of PRS, who argues that after 37 years the law has not delivered stable governments and has weakened the Speaker's office. In his reading, its real effect is to hand party leadership a whip over dissent. He notes that it applies even to Rajya Sabha members and members of legislative councils, who play no part in forming governments. He points to the United States and the United Kingdom, which have no such law, where parties manage dissent through internal mechanisms, and to Pakistan's Article 63A, which confines the restriction to resignation or voting against the party on confidence and budget votes. His sharpest line is a question: why should the Constitution protect parties that cannot keep their members together?

Both readings can be true at once. The 1967 evidence suggests that unregulated switching rewards office-seeking over mandate. The post-1985 evidence suggests that regulating it moved the contest to new places: group splits until 2003, mergers afterwards, and delay or doubt about what counts as joining another party. It also suggests that a rule is only as good as its umpire. A deadline from the Supreme Court can make a Speaker decide, but not decide in a way that satisfies the aggrieved party.

Gaya Lal never needed a theory of any of this. He had a constituency, a calendar of nine hours, and a press conference waiting in Chandigarh. What the pun preserved is the uncomfortable fact that a voter chooses a person and a symbol together, and a legislator can separate them in an afternoon. Every reform since has been an attempt to decide whose claim on that seat is stronger: the voter's, the party's, or the member's own conscience. Almost sixty years on, India has not settled it, and the next defection dispute will arrive with the same old question wearing new clothes.
