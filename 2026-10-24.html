The Man Who Bet Against the World's First Public Company

A ban that never settled the argument

On 27 February 1610, an ordinance in the Dutch Republic made it illegal to sell shares you did not own. It is one of the earliest known attempts to regulate trading on a stock market, and it was aimed at a practice that a small group of Amsterdam merchants, led by Isaac Le Maire, stood accused of using against the most powerful company of its day.

The argument that ordinance began has never really ended. In September 2008, in the middle of the global financial crisis, the US Securities and Exchange Commission temporarily banned short sales of 799 financial stocks, and regulators in the UK, Germany, France and several other countries followed with restrictions of their own. Studies afterwards found that the bans had little effect on how prices moved, while reducing trading volume and liquidity. Every few years, in every market, the same question returns. Is the short seller a parasite who profits from other people's misery, or the one honest voice in a room full of optimists?

Le Maire is worth meeting because his case refuses to settle the question. He was a founding shareholder with a real grievance, a critic of the company's governance, and a man who, by the charge brought against him, tried to push a share price down for his own gain. Historians still argue about which of those he was. The most useful answer may be that he was all of them at once.

A company whose owners could not get their money back

To see why a short seller could even exist in 1609, start with what the Dutch had invented seven years earlier. On 20 March 1602 the States General granted a charter to the United East India Company, the VOC, which gave it a 21-year monopoly on Dutch trade in Asia. Until then, Dutch voyages to the East had been funded one expedition at a time: investors put in money, the ships sailed, and when they returned the profits were divided and the partnership dissolved. The VOC broke with that pattern. It raised a permanent capital of roughly 6.4 million guilders from 1,143 subscribers across six regional chambers, and the money was locked in. An investor could not ask the company for it back. The only way out was to sell the share to someone else.

That single rule is what created a stock market. Once capital is permanent and shares are transferable, a price appears, and once a price appears, people begin to have opinions about where it is heading. Open-air trading in VOC shares grew in Amsterdam, and a dedicated exchange building on the Rokin canal followed by 1611. Forward contracts, options and short sales developed alongside it.

Isaac Le Maire was there at the beginning. Born around 1558 or 1559 in a family of merchants from Tournai, he was a prosperous grocer and a militia captain in Antwerp until the city fell to the Spanish in 1585, after which he fled north and settled in Amsterdam. In 1599 he helped found the Brabant Company, which sent ships to the Indies, and when the VOC was formed he became one of its directors and a major subscriber. Sources differ on the size of his stake: one scholarly account gives 60,000 guilders, while another popular reference gives 85,000.

Then it went wrong. In early 1605 he left the board amid allegations of embezzlement. On 22 February 1605 he signed a declaration and pledged 3,000 Flemish pounds as a guarantee, and he agreed not to compete with the company in Asian trade. Whether he was guilty or was pushed out in a boardroom fight is not something the surviving record settles. What is clear is that he never forgave the men who had forced him out, and that they were now his opponents in a dispute that would run for the rest of his life.

The secret society of 11 February 1609

Le Maire's campaign had two faces, and it helps to keep them apart.

The first was a complaint. On 24 January 1609 he sent a paper to Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, the most powerful statesman of the Republic, opposing an extension of the directors' privileges. Accounts of his arguments describe a company that carried debt that made dividends hard, that did not listen to its shareholders, and whose directors seemed reluctant to publish accounts. Oldenbarnevelt rejected the complaints. These were the same governance questions that shareholder activists raise today, and they were raised four centuries ago by a man with a personal score to settle.

The second was a wager. On 11 February 1609, Le Maire and eight other merchants formed a secret partnership, known in the sources as the "big company", to trade in VOC shares. Le Maire held four-fifteenths of it. Nobody has ever found the founding document; historians infer its existence from legal acts of 1610 and 1612. Its method, as the directors later described it, was to sell shares for future delivery in quantities larger than the partners held. If the price fell before delivery, they could buy the shares cheaper and pocket the difference. That is a short sale, and because the shares were not owned when sold, what we would now call a naked one.

The price did drift down. According to the price series assembled in a chapter by the finance scholar Poitras, VOC shares stood at about 158 to 160 per cent of par in 1607 and were quoted at 126 per cent for cash in January 1610. Whether the partners caused that slide is the contested part. The directors thought so. They petitioned the States for a ban, calling it a "dirty scheme" of men selling shares they did not hold. Several accounts add that rumours of shipwrecks were spread to help the price along, though the surviving evidence on that point is thinner than the story suggests. A group of businessmen answered that the decline had natural causes: islands lost, a failed siege, ships sunk.

On 27 February 1610 the ordinance came. It banned blank sales and required that shares sold be transferred within a month. The directors had wanted the rule to apply to contracts already made; that request was left out.

The squeeze, the exile and the voyage round the Horn

The ban did not end Le Maire's story in a courtroom. It ended it in the market, which is where short sellers are most fragile.

A short seller has promised to deliver something he does not have, and the promise gets more expensive if the price goes up. After the ordinance, VOC shares rose. The scholar's price series puts them at about 200 per cent of par by spring 1611, against 126 per cent for cash in January 1610. The partners had contracts to deliver shares at old, lower prices. In April and May 1610, four members of the group, Hans Bouwer, Harmen Rosenerans, Reinier Lems and Jacques Damman, went bankrupt, and Hans and Jasper Bouwer were later convicted of fraud. A recent article in the Economic History Review by Oscar Gelderblom, Abe de Jong and Joost Jonker is titled "The first bear raid and squeeze", which captures the shape of the episode: a raid on the price, then a squeeze on the raiders.

Le Maire himself survived financially, but not comfortably. In spring 1611 he left Amsterdam for Egmond, and the directors went on refusing to release the 3,000 pounds he had pledged in 1605. In 1613 he asked for compensation and claimed losses of more than 1.5 million guilders over his career, though the source that records this is inconsistent about the figure, and his gravestone is itself a puzzle. It reads "more than 150,000" guilders over thirty years, which one older historian argued was a dropped zero, meant to read 1.5 million. Nobody can now say which is right.

What he did next is the part that tends to be forgotten. In 1614 he founded the Australian Company to find a way into the Pacific that avoided the two routes the VOC monopoly covered: the Cape of Good Hope and the Strait of Magellan. His son Jacob and the experienced captain Willem Schouten sailed from Texel on 14 June 1615 with two ships, the Eendracht and the Hoorn, and found the passage round Cape Horn. They did not find the southern continent they were looking for. The VOC, learning of the venture's real purpose, had the ships seized in the East Indies. A court ruled in 1619 that the seizure of the Eendracht had been unlawful. Isaac died on 20 September 1624, still litigating, and the Australian Company's case was finally rejected by the States General in 1644.

What a 1609 bet still teaches

The first lesson is about design. A market that lets you buy a claim on a company's future has to let you sell it as well, or else the price reflects only the views of the people who are enthusiastic. Permanent capital made the VOC a company that could last. It also made the share price into a public opinion poll, and a poll that only the optimistic can vote in is a poor one.

The second is that the same act can be two different things. A short position taken because you have found real trouble in a company is research expressed as a trade. A short position held while you spread rumours of sinking ships is manipulation. The trade looks identical on the ledger. The difference is in whether what you say is true, and in 1609 there was no regulator to ask. The honest record is that Le Maire did both kinds of things at different times: his complaint to Oldenbarnevelt was a governance argument, while the rumour campaign described in several accounts, even if thinly evidenced, was the opposite.

The third is about who asks for the rules. The 1610 ordinance was requested by the directors, the very people whose conduct Le Maire had attacked, and their appeal rested on the harm to ordinary shareholders, whom they called widows and orphans. That does not make the ban wrong. Rumour-driven price falls do hurt real people. But it is a reminder that the loudest demand for a ban on short selling often comes from the people being shorted, and a good rule should be judged by its evidence and not by who asked for it. The later studies of the 2008 bans, which found little effect on prices, suggest that caution is warranted.
