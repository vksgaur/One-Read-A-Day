The Smallest Library Among My Kin: Ahmad Baba and the Fall of Timbuktu

Ahmad Baba al-Timbukti (1556 to 1627) and the Moroccan conquest that scattered the scholars of Timbuktu.

A man with 1,600 books and a complaint

The most famous sentence attributed to Ahmad Baba is a piece of understatement so dry that it deserves to be read twice. Brought before the Sultan of Morocco as a captive, he is reported to have said: "I have the smallest library among my kin and you have stolen from me more than 1600 books." That line appears in the key-dates chronology published by the Tombouctou Manuscripts Project at the University of Cape Town, under the year 1593. I could not trace it to a primary Arabic source, so treat it as a tradition reliably repeated rather than a verified transcript. Even so, it captures something true about the world he came from.

Consider the arithmetic. Sixteen hundred volumes, in the 1590s, were all handwritten. Each one had to be copied, bound, and in many cases carried across the Sahara. That is a fortune in labour alone. Yet in Baba's telling, a collection of that size made him a modest member of his family circle, not a prodigy. The same Cape Town project describes a golden age of Timbuktu scholarship that ran through the reign of Askiya al-Hajj Muhammad (1493 to 1528) and says it was "abruptly halted" by the Moroccan invasion of 1591.

The project also records that much of Baba's library was destroyed in the invasion. So the sentence has two layers. It is a complaint, and it is a census: a conquered man counting what the conquerors took, and by implication, what they failed to understand.

This article follows Ahmad Baba through three acts that history can document: the Timbuktu that made him, the invasion that unmade it, and the exile in which he kept working. It tries to be careful along the way. The sources for this period are thin and they disagree on some dates, so I will say where they do.

The city that made him

Ahmad Baba was born on 26 October 1556 in Araouane, a desert settlement north of Timbuktu, into the Aqit family, a lineage of Sanhaja Berber jurists. Timbuktu's own chronology records that Mahmud b. Umar Aqit, the first known imam of the Sankore mosque, died in 1548, eight years before Baba's birth. Scholarship was close to a family trade.

He moved to Timbuktu while young and studied first with his father, then with the scholar Mohammed Bagayogo. He belonged to the Maliki school of Sunni jurisprudence, the school that dominated West Africa and North Africa. Britannica describes him as coming from a family of jurists and educated in Islamic jurisprudence and culture, and the Cape Town project counts more than 56 works to his name, though Wikipedia says more than 40. Counts of an author's books depend on what survives and how one counts, so I would simply say: dozens.

Timbuktu in these decades was not a remote curiosity. It sat at the southern end of the trans-Saharan trade, in the Songhai empire, and its scholars were part of a conversation that ran from the Niger bend to Fez and Cairo. The same Cape Town project says that Askiya Daoud, who ruled from 1548 to 1583, reportedly established public libraries in his kingdom. A reader today might imagine the manuscripts of Timbuktu as relics. In Baba's time they were working tools: legal opinions, commentaries, grammars, and the accounts of merchants who needed contracts they could trust.

It matters for what follows that this was a Muslim state with Muslim scholars. When the Moroccan sultan decided to take it, he was attacking a fellow Muslim kingdom, and the people he would eventually deport were the guardians of the very law he claimed to uphold.

Gunpowder and a mistaken belief about gold

The conquest began with a treasury problem. According to the standard account summarised in the Battle of Tondibi article, Sultan Ahmad al-Mansur of Morocco was short of money after the defences he built following the Battle of Alcacer Quibir had strained his finances toward bankruptcy. He also believed, wrongly, that the Songhai grew rich from gold mines of their own. Some of his advisers warned that attacking another Muslim state was unlawful. He overruled them.

The army he sent south under Judar Pasha was small. The Tondibi article gives about 1,500 light cavalry and 2,500 infantry, with the infantry armed largely with arquebuses and the force supported by a handful of cannon (sources vary between six and eight). Half the infantry were converts and half were exiles from the former Emirate of Granada. The Songhai army opposing it was much larger. Estimates differ widely: the Tarikh al-Fattash gives 18,000 cavalry and 9,700 foot soldiers, while other estimates run as high as 80,000 in total. What the Songhai lacked was gunpowder weapons.

On 13 March 1591, at Tondibi just north of Gao, the two armies met. The Songhai tried to stampede about a thousand cattle into the Moroccan lines. Gunfire and cannon noise turned the animals back onto the Songhai themselves. Arquebus fire drove back the infantry and broke the cavalry charge, and the whole battle lasted about two hours. Casualty figures are unknown.

What followed was less glorious than the victory. Judar Pasha took Gao, whose residents had fled, and found little wealth. He moved on to Timbuktu and Djenne, richer trading cities, and looted all three. The mistaken belief in gold mines was an expensive one for the people who lived in these towns. Morocco never managed firm control of so vast a territory with such long supply lines, and a decade of sporadic fighting followed. The Songhai empire effectively ended as a regional power, and the surviving Songhai withdrew east to Dendi, where they kept their traditions alive for about two and a half centuries.

As for Timbuktu, the Cape Town project writes that the Moroccan army "conquers and destroys the Songhay state to control the lucrative Saharan trade." The Moroccan campaign was an economic raid that became an occupation, and an occupying army needs the cooperation, or at least the silence, of the people whose opinions carry weight. In Timbuktu, those people were the scholars.

Exile, and the work that survived it

Britannica says that after the conquest Ahmad Baba was accused of refusing to recognise the Moroccan sultan's authority and of plotting a rebellion. Whether the charge was true is unknown, and I found nothing in the sources I could open that settles it. What is documented is the outcome: he was removed from Timbuktu and taken to Morocco.

The date is where the sources split. Britannica and Wikipedia give 1594 for his deportation. The Cape Town chronology records his exile to Marrakesh under 1593, and also lists 1593 to 1608 as the span of his exile. I have used both years in this article where the source I am citing uses them, because I cannot responsibly choose between them. Place is a smaller disagreement: Britannica and the Cape Town project name Marrakesh, while Wikipedia says he remained in Fez until the sultan's death. A university research report from Brandenburg Technical University says he taught in both cities, which is the safest reading.

The striking thing is what he was allowed to do there. Britannica states that he was permitted to teach and to practise law. His fatwas from this period are noted for their clarity, and he compiled a biographical dictionary of Maliki jurists that remains an important source for historians. Wikipedia lists Nayl al-Ibtihaj among his notable works, without describing its contents, and says much of his noted writing was produced in Morocco. In other words, a state that had looted his city and taken his books then relied on his legal mind. That is less contradictory than it sounds, since any government that rules a new population needs people who know how law is argued.

His release came with a change of reign. Ahmad al-Mansur died in 1603, and Britannica says Baba was then allowed to return. Wikipedia adds that the new sultan, Zaydan an-Nasser, allowed exiles to go home, and gives the date of Baba's arrival in Timbuktu as 22 April 1608. The Cape Town chronology says 1607. The difference is only a year, but it is another reminder that the written record of West African history in this era was assembled later, from fragments.

A difficult legacy, and an honest one

Baba's best-known argument is also his most uncomfortable for modern readers. His treatise Mi'raj al-Su'ud, written in response to questions about enslavement, aimed to protect Muslims from being enslaved by other Muslims. A research report summarising the scholarship says it argued that Muslims cannot be enslaved regardless of skin colour, while non-Muslims could still be enslaved under Maliki law. The Ottoman History Podcast describes him as formulating an argument against enslavement based on race or skin colour. Wikipedia adds two cautions: that he criticised the association of Black Africans with slavery and the "Curse of Ham" story, and that he did not oppose the slave trade in general, with the historian William Phillips characterising his position as based on religion, not race. Both things are true at once. Baba was a progressive within his legal tradition and a man of his century, and a writer who tries to make him either a saint or a villain will have to ignore half the evidence.

He lived the rest of his life in Timbuktu, teaching and writing, and died there on 22 April 1627, aged 70. His name outlived him in a form he would probably have appreciated. The Ahmed Baba Institute, founded in 1973 and now housed in a building inaugurated opposite the Sankore mosque in 2009, bears his name and cares for manuscripts. Wikipedia says it holds over 18,000. The Cape Town project estimates that about 300,000 manuscripts survive in circulation in and around Timbuktu, a figure that shows how deeply the habit of copying and keeping books ran in the city even after the shock of 1591. A crater on Mercury is also named after him.

What the library line teaches

The tradition about the 1,600 stolen books lasts because it is funny, and because it is a clean description of a mistake that empires repeatedly make. Al-Mansur sent an army to find gold and found a city whose wealth was mostly in paper, ink and trained memory, things a column of arquebusiers cannot carry home. The Moroccans did take Baba, and they did get some useful legal work out of him. They did not get a Timbuktu that functioned as before, and the Cape Town project's phrase is that the city's importance "gradually declined" afterward.

For anyone who loves books and history, the lesson is modest. Knowledge is a form of capital, and like all capital it can be destroyed by people who do not know what they are looking at. It also travels. A scholar in forced exile in Marrakesh or Fez was still, on the evidence, productive. What he carried in his head turned out to be harder to confiscate than what he kept on his shelves.

I will end where the sources end, with their disagreements: 1593 or 1594, Marrakesh or Fez, 1607 or 1608. Honest history of this period is often like that. It offers a firm outline, a few precise dates, one marvellous sentence, and a reminder that someone, long ago, counted his books and found the number was too small.
